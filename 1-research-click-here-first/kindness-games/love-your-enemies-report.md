# Love your enemies as a game: how exits and sample games and previews and visible records change whether kindness survives

An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this report on 15 November 2026 from small computer tournaments that the chatbot ran the same day. No other person has rechecked the report. The owner of this repository proposed every idea that was tested which means the megaphone argument and the exit and the sample game and the preview buffer and the visible record. The tournaments are toys with numbers the chatbot chose so the results show a direction and prove nothing about real people.

## The idea that started the tests

The owner of this repository pointed out a property of the instructions "love your enemies and bless those who curse you and forgive those who wrong you." Most battle plans fail the moment the other side reads the plan. A person can shout "love your enemies" through a megaphone because an enemy who steals those orders and obeys the orders has stopped being an enemy. So the plan needs no secrecy.

The question for the tests was what kind of surroundings let a player who follows that plan do well and not get drained.

## How the game works

- Two players meet and each player chooses a kind move or a harmful move on every turn.
- Two kind moves pay 3 points to each player.
- A harmful move against a kind move pays 5 points to the harmer and 0 points to the kind player.
- Two harmful moves pay 1 point to each player.
- A meeting lasts up to 20 turns. Finding a new partner costs 1 turn. Each player has a limited number of turns which stands for limited time and energy.
- The score for a type of player is the average number of points per turn. A score of 3 is the most a pair of kind players can earn together turn after turn.

## The players

- The always-kind player makes a kind move every turn and never leaves a partner. The always-kind player stands for loving an enemy with no protection at all.
- The mirroring player copies whatever the partner did on the previous turn and never leaves.
- The open harmer makes a harmful move every turn.
- Other harmers appear in single tests and each test describes the harmer where the harmer appears.

## Test 1: no tools and then an exit

With no tools and half the crowd made of open harmers the always-kind player scored 1.40 which was the lowest score. The open harmer scored 1.55 and the mirroring player scored 1.86.

Then kind players were allowed to leave a partner after the partner's first harmful move. The kind player with an exit scored 2.64 which was the highest score in the crowd. The same kind moves that scored last without an exit scored first with an exit.

- The exit worked because of limited time and energy. A mirroring player stays locked in a long exchange of harm with a malicious partner. A player who leaves spends those same turns with a kind partner.
- Leaving after one harm scored 2.64 and leaving after two harms scored 2.53 and leaving after three harms scored 2.45. The gaps are small and came from a single run.
- The exit left one gap. When one player in ten was a harmer the harmers scored highest at 3.30 because a rare harmer keeps finding fresh kind partners and takes one large payoff from each partner before the partner leaves. This report calls that a hit and run.

## Test 2: a sample game at one tenth of the stakes

Either player could ask for one sample turn before the real game. The sample turn paid one tenth of the normal points. A player who was harmed in the sample turn walked away.

- The sample game rescued the always-kind player who never leaves. With half the crowd made of open harmers that player rose from 1.41 to 2.51 because every open harmer was caught at a tenth of the cost.
- With the mild penalty above a plain exit scored slightly higher (2.57 against 2.50). The sample turn spends a turn on every partner including the kind partners while an exit spends a turn only after harm happens.
- The penalty for being harmed was then raised from 0 points to minus 10 points. The sample game player then scored 2.47 and the plain exit player scored 2.15. So a cheap rehearsal is worth a turn when a real blow is heavy.
- The sample game left one gap. A disguised harmer who plays kind in the sample turn and harms in the real game passed the test. A kind player who trusted the sample game and never left afterward fell to 1.39 against a crowd that was half disguised harmers. A kind player who used the sample game and also kept the exit held at 2.41.

## Test 3: a buffer that shows the other player's move before the move is played

On each turn both players post an intended move and both players see both posts. A kind player who reads the buffer can veto the turn which means nobody is paid and both players stay. A kind player can also leave.

When the posted move must be the move played:

- A harmer earned nothing from a player who read the buffer because every posted harm was vetoed or walked away from.
- A selfish learner was added. The selfish learner tries harm first and switches to kind moves once a harm attempt is vetoed. Against buffer readers the selfish learner played kind for the remaining turns because kind moves were the one way left to earn points.
- With half the crowd made of harmers the kind player who vetoed and stayed scored 2.63 and the kind player who left at once scored 2.61. The patient veto kept the reformed partner.
- Harmers still scored about 3.0 to 3.1 when harmers were one in ten. All of that income came from kind players who ignored the buffer.

When a player could post a kind move and then play a harmful move:

- The buffer added nothing over a plain exit. Buffer readers and plain exit players both scored 2.61.
- A lying harmer scored highest at 3.42 when harmers were one in ten.

So a truthful preview was the strongest tool tested because harm became impossible and not merely punished afterward. Everything depended on the preview being true.

## Test 4: a visible record and the choice to decline a match

Every player carried a visible record of the player's last twenty moves. A checking player declined any partner whose record showed harm on more than half of those moves.

- The owner of this repository predicted that an always-kind player would do well by seeing that the next partner harms most of the time and declining the match. The prediction held. The always-kind player who never leaves rose from 2.17 to 2.62 with half the crowd made of harmers and from 1.75 to 2.41 with eight in ten made of harmers. The player's moves never changed.
- Checking the record and also keeping the exit scored best among kind players at 2.76 because the exit covers partners whose record is too short to judge.
- Open harmers still scored highest in the mixed crowd at 3.27. All of that income came from kind players who skipped the record check. A kind player who never checks and never leaves hands a harmer twenty paid turns in a row.
- When every kind player checked records and harmers were one in ten the open harmer fell to last place at 2.03.
- A careful harmer who harms four turns in ten stayed under the cutoff and scored about as well as the kind players (2.86 against 2.84). A fixed cutoff tells a harmer how much harm goes unanswered.
- A selfish learner who switched to kind moves after being declined three times was exploited after reforming because the reformed player did not check records.

## What the four tests say together

- The kind player's moves never had to change. In every test the kind player kept making kind moves and the whole gain came from choosing whom to play with.
- Each tool lifted the kind player a great deal. The always-kind player with no tools scored last and a kind player with any one tool scored far above that player in every test.
- Each tool protected the players who used the tool. Harmers lived on the kind players who used no tool.
- Each tool had one gap. The exit allowed hit and run. The sample game allowed disguise. The buffer allowed lying when posts were not binding. The record check allowed harm just under the cutoff.
- Stacking tools closed gaps. The sample game plus the exit handled disguised harmers. The record check plus the exit handled partners with short records.
- Surroundings decided the outcome more than character did. The same always-kind player was the biggest loser in one setting and near the top in another setting.

## How "love your enemies" shows up in the game

- Loving an enemy in the game is the kind move made every turn toward every partner including a partner who harmed in the past.
- The veto is the closest thing in the game to naming a harm while respecting the person because a veto says "not that move and I am still here." The veto was the tool that turned a selfish player into a cooperating partner.
- The exit is not hatred. A player who leaves a partner who keeps harming still made no harmful move and saved turns for partners who can receive kindness.
- The megaphone argument held in every test. A kind player loses nothing by announcing "I make kind moves and I check records and I leave when harmed" because a harmer who hears the announcement gains the most by making kind moves too.

## What the tests could mean for food and shelter and deep human connection

The tests are about points and the reader's life is about three needs which are food and shelter and deep meaningful human connection. Each tool has a plain human form and each human form has a cost.

- An exit could look like ending a conversation with a person who keeps using dehumanizing words. The cost is the loss of whatever that person might have offered later.
- A sample game could look like a short first conversation before sharing a home or money with a new person. The cost is time spent on every new person including the kind ones.
- A buffer could look like two people saying out loud what each person plans to do before doing the plan such as "I plan to ask you about your week and then tell you about my loneliness." The buffer works only between people who then do what was said.
- A visible record could look like asking mutual acquaintances how a person has treated others. The cost is that a record can be wrong or out of date and a person who has changed can be turned away unfairly.

A person short on time and energy could keep offering kindness to everybody and still choose where the limited hours go.

## Limits of the tests

- The points and the player types and the cutoff of one half are choices the chatbot made. Different choices could change which player scores highest.
- Test 1 came from a single run. Tests 2 and 3 and 4 are averages of five runs. Small gaps between scores should not be trusted.
- The harmers follow fixed rules. A real harmer could learn and adapt in ways the tests do not cover.
- No player is ever truly hurt in the tests. A zero in a table is not hunger and a real person harmed even once can lose food or shelter.
- Researchers have studied games like these for decades and the report did not survey that research. Some of these results may already be well known.
