# Where was the Voynich manuscript made: a clue-by-clue location guess

This page treats the Voynich manuscript like a round of a map-guessing game which means every drawing detail is read as a clue about where and when the makers lived.
An AI model (Claude) gathered the clues on 11 November 2026 from published descriptions by researchers and the AI model did not look at the page pictures directly which means every clue below rests on what other people reported seeing.
Several sources are blogs and forums run by long-time hobby researchers and not journals which means a careful reader could treat the clue list as leads to check and not as settled facts.

## The verdict first

Here is the best guess: the makers probably worked in the borderland around the Alps where northern Italian and southern German habits of drawing met which means somewhere in the band from northern Italy up through the Tyrol and the Lake Constance region.

- The castle clue and the plant-book clue point to northern Italy.
- The star-sign clues point to German-speaking lands.
- A workshop in the Alpine borderland could explain both sets of clues because artists there could see Italian castles and German calendars.
- The date clues agree with each other because the clothing fashions fit the years 1400 to 1430 and the laboratory dating of the pages gave 1404 to 1438.
- The guess is moderate and not firm because every clue can be copied from an older picture by an artist living somewhere else.

## Clue 1: the castle with forked wall tops

- What researchers see: a small castle on the large fold-out page has wall tops shaped like the forked tail of a swallow.
- Where the clue points: northern Italy and the nearby Alps because the forked shape began as a badge of one political side in the Italian city wars and still stands on castles from the Aosta valley to Verona.
- How strong: moderate because collectors of old coats of arms report the forked shape as common in Italian sources and uncommon in German sources and absent from the French and English sources checked so far.
- What could fool a guesser: old manuscripts sometimes show forked wall tops in countries that never built forked wall tops which means the artist could have copied a picture and never seen such a castle.
- A second limit: many castles got forked wall tops during repairs centuries later so a castle that has forked wall tops today does not prove the same castle had forked wall tops before 1450.

## Clue 2: the archer with legs and a crossbow

- What researchers see: the star sign Sagittarius is drawn as a standing man with two legs who holds a crossbow where most old star-sign pictures show a creature that is half man and half horse with a longbow.
- Where the clue points: German-speaking lands because a researcher who compared the whole set of star-sign pictures reported the crossbow version only in works from German-speaking areas.
- A narrower pointer: a second researcher who mapped the human archer pictures reported a cluster in southern Germany and northern Switzerland.
- A date pointer: the same researcher reported that most pictures had gone back to longbows by the middle of the 1400s which fits a book made in the early 1400s.
- How strong: moderate because the comparison sets were built by hobby researchers and nobody has published a full count in a journal.

## Clue 3: the crayfish and the courting couple

- What researchers see: the star sign Cancer is drawn as a crayfish or lobster and the star sign Gemini is drawn as a clothed man and woman holding hands where the older tradition shows two male twins.
- Where the clue points: Europe north of the Alps because one survey of several hundred old star-sign sets found the crayfish in about three quarters of the German examples while French examples mixed crabs and crayfish.
- The couple pointer: the same survey reported that the closest matches for the clothed couple come from the lands of the old German empire.
- How strong: weak to moderate because the crayfish also appears outside Germany and the couple is described as uncommon everywhere.

## Clue 4: the baggy sleeves

- What researchers see: the archer wears a tunic with sleeves that are baggy at the elbow and tight at the wrist and the women in the star-sign pages wear dresses with long cut sleeves.
- Where the clue points: the years 1400 to 1430 because researchers who collected dated pictures found the baggy-elbow sleeve all over European book painting in those three decades and rarely afterward.
- How strong: fairly strong as a date clue because the clothing date and the laboratory date of 1404 to 1438 were reached by unrelated methods and overlap.
- What the clue cannot do: the baggy sleeve was an international fashion which means the sleeve gives a date and does not give a place.
- A disagreement to report: one forum writer judged the women's dress to be closest to French fashion which is an opinion that does not fit the German star-sign clues.

## Clue 5: the plant pictures

- What researchers see: the plant pages resemble a family of plant books made in northern Italy in the 1400s for physicians and pharmacists.
- Where the clue points: northern Italy.
- How strong: moderate because the resemblance is a judgment about drawing style and the Voynich plants themselves match no known species.

## Clue 6: the month names written in ordinary letters

- What researchers see: someone wrote month names such as "aberil" and "augst" and "octembre" beside the star signs in ordinary Latin letters and in darker ink than the main writing.
- What the darker ink suggests: a later reader added the month names which means the month names tell where an early owner came from and not where the makers lived.
- Where the clue points: a speaker of a French-like language because researchers have matched the spellings to southern French and Catalan and northern French and nobody has settled which.
- One concrete match: the odd spelling "octembre" also appears on a brass star-measuring tool from Picardy in northern France from the 1300s.
- How strong: weak for locating the makers and useful for tracing where the book traveled.

## Clue 7: the German words on the last page

- What researchers see: the last page has a few lines in ordinary letters that include "so nim" which was a very common opening in German recipes ("so take") and "pox leber" which means the liver of a male goat.
- Where the clue points: a German-speaking writer and one researcher places the handwriting style mainly in northeast Switzerland and southwest Germany.
- What the clue suggests: a later owner probably wrote the lines because researchers judge the handwriting to differ from the main scribes which again describes where the book traveled.
- How strong: moderate for "a German speaker held the book early" and weak for "the makers spoke German".

## Clue 8: the trail of owners

- The earliest probable owner was a physician and alchemist in Augsburg in southern Germany and the first confirmed owner was an alchemist in Prague.
- Where the clue points: the book was in German-speaking and Czech lands by about 1600 which fits a start somewhere in or near the Alps and does not prove such a start because books travel.

## Guesses that the clues argue against

- A claim that the book came from Mexico fits poorly because the pages were dated to 1404 through 1438 and Europeans first reached Mexico in 1519 and the star-sign pictures follow European calendar habits.
- A purely southern Italian or Spanish origin fits poorly because the crossbow archer has not been reported from those regions.

## What would make the guess firmer

- A journal-quality count of every dated star-sign set with a crossbow archer could confirm or break the German pointer.
- A list of castles proven to have forked wall tops before 1440 could narrow the castle clue from a whole region to a few valleys.
- A match between the handwriting of the main scribes and dated documents from one town would outweigh every picture clue because handwriting is tied to where a scribe trained.
