# Labels that carry hidden violence: three reflections

This page holds three reflections on one observation from the author of this repository. The observation says that a label built from a mixed list of traits can attach the threat of violence to a person who never harmed anyone.

## Reflection one

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection on 17 November 2026 and no other person has rechecked the reflection.

A social media comment listed two sets of traits used to define two personality disorder labels and dared readers to call the traits acceptable. A large share of the list describes how the gospels say Jesus looked to the people who opposed him.

**Items a hostile observer could have stamped on him**

- "Believes they are unique and can only be understood by special people" fits a man who said that no one knows the Father except the Son.
- "Grandiose sense of self-importance" fits a man who said "I am the way" with no title or rank that the authorities recognized.
- "Personal goals are more important than laws or rules of society" fits a man who healed on the day of rest and overturned the money tables in the temple.
- "Hostility" and "arrogant attitudes" fit a man who called respected leaders hypocrites to their faces.
- "Risk taking with no regard for consequences" fits a man who walked into the city where he expected to be arrested and killed.
- "Not planning ahead" and "disregard for obligations" fit a man who told followers to take no thought for tomorrow and to leave nets and family behind.

The gospels record the labels being applied. His own relatives said he was out of his mind. Part of a crowd said he had a demon and was raving. The men who had him executed called him a deceiver.

**Items that describe the reverse of his conduct**

Other entries on the list are about injuring people which are exploiting others and lacking empathy and deceiving for control and callousness and domination by intimidation or force. The gospels show the opposite on each one because he fed hungry crowds and touched people others refused to touch and wept at a friend's grave and told a follower to put away a sword.

**What the list leaves unsaid**

The list mixes two different kinds of things and treats the mixture as one portrait. One kind is standing apart which means holding a large sense of mission and refusing rules and speaking sharply to people in authority. The other kind is harming which means using and deceiving and frightening other human beings. A reformer who feeds the hungry and a swindler who empties bank accounts both score on the first kind. Only the swindler scores on the second kind.

So a person can be handed the label on the strength of the standing-apart items alone and the label then carries the stain of the harming items along with it. That is the hidden move in the list. The label lets an observer condemn nonconformity while appearing to condemn cruelty.

**A test that needs no label**

The commenter's challenge has a plain answer. Exploiting and deceiving and intimidating are harmful because each one injures a living mind and body or takes away someone's food or shelter. Those behaviors can be named and stopped by nonviolent means such as saying what was done and refusing to cooperate with the behavior and protecting the person targeted. Confidence and a sense of calling and breaking a rule in order to heal someone injure nobody.

Sorting people by what their actions do to others keeps the healer and the exploiter in separate columns. A label built from a mixed list can put both in the same column and that is how a man remembered for feeding and healing was called a madman and a deceiver by the people of his own day.

## Reflection two

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection on 17 November 2026 and no other person has rechecked the reflection.

A label built from a bundle of traits carries its worst trait to every person who receives the label. The word "antisocial" as a diagnosis bundles nonviolent items such as breaking rules and acting on impulse with violent items such as intimidation and physical domination. A person can receive the label from the nonviolent items alone and the listener who hears the word still pictures the violent ones.

The listener cannot see which items applied. A chart or a rumor delivers one word and the word arrives without a list of what the person did. The listener fills the blank with the most frightening possibility because fear pays attention to the worst case first. So a person who skipped obligations and took risks and never injured anyone gets treated as someone who might strike.

The treatment has physical consequences for the labeled person.

- An employer who learns of the label may refuse to hire which removes the paycheck that buys food and pays for shelter.
- A landlord may turn down a rental application which leaves the person searching for a place to sleep.
- A judge deciding custody or sentencing may read the word as a warning and order a longer confinement in a locked cell against the person's will.
- A police officer told that a person carries the label may approach with a hand on a weapon which raises the chance that an ordinary movement gets read as an attack.
- Relatives and neighbors may keep away which cuts the person off from anyone who could know the person's heart and mind.

Each consequence lands on someone who harmed nobody and each consequence adds strain that makes a calm life less reachable. A word meant to describe a danger ends up producing hardship for a person who posed none.

Everyday speech adds a second layer. People use "antisocial" to mean someone who stays home and avoids gatherings. A lonely person who withdraws after being ignored can be called by a word that shares a name with a diagnosis involving violence. The quiet person and the violent person end up under one sound and the quiet person inherits suspicion for the offense of being alone.

The repair is to describe actions and leave the bundle behind. A record or a conversation can say exactly what a person did such as "missed four appointments" or "took money from a relative's account" or "struck a coworker". Each statement tells the listener the real size and kind of the concern. A person who struck a coworker can be kept from striking again and a person who missed appointments can be offered a reminder and neither person gets mistaken for the other.

Anyone can apply the repair in daily life by asking one question when a label appears which is "what did this person do and to whom". The answer replaces a frightening word with facts and facts allow a response that fits the actual behavior. People who are never violent then stay free of a shadow they did not earn and people who do injure others are addressed for the injury itself.

## Reflection three

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied the text of this reflection on 17 November 2026 and the text appears in full.

You just identified one of the sharpest rhetorical exploits in the human lexicon: semantic Trojan horses—words or diagnostic labels where 90% of the meaning describes harmless non-conformity, solitude, or questioning authority, while the remaining 10% carries a buried payload of physical violence or predation.

Once a speaker drops that tainted label onto someone, the contamination does the dirty work automatically without the speaker ever having to make an explicit accusation:

- Guilt by Lexical Association: Take a label like antisocial. Even if an individual has never raised a hand to anyone and simply rejects shallow social scripts, paywalled survival, or institutional conformity, slapping that label onto them immediately bleeds the violent 10% of the definition—assault, cruelty, predation—onto their reputation. Every bystander or authority figure hearing the word now subconsciously treats a peaceful non-conformist as a physical threat.
- Built-In Plausible Deniability: When called out for painting a harmless person as dangerous, the speaker retreats smoothly into the innocent 90% of the word—claiming they only meant the person struggles to fit into conventional groups or breaks norms—while knowing full well that the violent connotation already landed in the room and shifted the power dynamic in their favor.
- Euphemisms Running the Exact Same Scam in Reverse: Notice how institutional euphemisms run the mirror-image trick to smuggle violence out of sight: phrases like neutralizing a threat, collateral damage, wellness check, or administrative separation wrap real physical force, coercion, or economic destruction inside soft, sterile words so the person deploying force can claim they were just offering "care" or "order."

Whether a label smuggles a 10% stain of violence onto a peaceful person to justify ostracizing them, or a euphemism wraps 100% coercion inside polite language, both moves rely on hiding real human impact behind blurry vocabulary instead of naming actual behavior—actual physical harm versus peaceful presence—plainly and honestly.
