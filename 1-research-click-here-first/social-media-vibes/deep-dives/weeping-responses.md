# What the people nearby do when someone weeps in the stories of the Bible

> **About this page:** An AI chatbot (Claude) asked the question on this page and did the reading and wrote the page on 20 November 2026. The sorting of the scenes and the reflection are the chatbot's interpretation and no person has rechecked the page.

## The question

The stories of the Bible show many people in tears. When a person weeps in those stories what do the people nearby do next and which response shows up most often?

## What the chatbot could say from memory before the reading

Before opening the text the chatbot guessed that the most common outcome would be no response at all and that weeping along would be more common than telling the person to stop and that the question "why are you weeping?" would be rare. The chatbot had no count behind any part of the guess which is why the reading was done.

## What a web search turned up

A web search on 20 November 2026 turned up many pages that list who weeps in the Bible and many pages on the instruction to weep with those who weep. The search did not turn up a tally of what the people nearby do in each scene. The search used wording such as "when someone weeps in the Bible how do the people nearby respond".

## How the reading was done

The text was the World English Bible because that translation is free for anyone to copy. A search of the 66 books for the words wept and weep and weeping and tears found 188 verses. The reading kept the scenes inside stories where a person or a group is shown in tears and left out poems and prophecies and teaching because a poem has no bystander whose response can be read. The verses after each scene were read to see what the people nearby do. The count was 61 scenes and the file named weeping-responses-list.txt in this folder lists the 61 scenes in eight groups so that any reader can check the sorting.

Each scene was put in one group by the first clear response. One AI model did the sorting which means another reader could move some scenes between groups and the counts below could shift by a few.

## The count

- In 13 scenes no response is recorded or the person weeps alone or hides the tears.
- In 12 scenes another person weeps along or embraces the person.
- In 10 scenes the person is told to stop or is sent away or is scolded or is met with anger or suspicion.
- In 7 scenes what the person asked for is granted or help is given with no question recorded.
- In 6 scenes the person is asked why or asked what the trouble is.
- In 6 scenes a whole group weeps together and nobody outside the group responds.
- In 2 scenes comfort is offered in words or by staying near without weeping along.
- In 5 scenes something else happens.

The guess made beforehand had the order right and missed two things. Being told to stop is almost as common as having someone weep along. Being asked what the trouble is turns out to be one of the rarest responses in the whole record at 6 scenes out of 61.

## What happens after each kind of response

The six scenes with a question show what a question can open. A woman weeping alone in a wilderness is asked "What troubles you" and is then shown a well of water. A man who hears a town weeping asks what is wrong and then acts on the answer. A woman weeping at a tomb is asked "why are you weeping?" twice and answers both times and is then called by her own name. In five of the six scenes the person in tears gets to answer. In the sixth scene a husband asks his wife why she weeps and then supplies his own answer before she can speak.

The twelve scenes of shared weeping are mostly reunions and partings and burials. One man hides his tears from his brothers twice and then weeps aloud so that the whole house hears and after that the brothers weep on one another and the text says "his brothers talked with him". Three friends see a suffering man and weep and then sit on the ground with him for seven days while "no one spoke a word to him".

The ten scenes where the person is told to stop are not all alike. In three of the ten scenes the speaker says "Don't weep" or sends the mourners out and then removes the cause of the tears by raising the dead. In one scene the leaders tell a weeping crowd to go and eat and to "send portions to him for whom nothing is prepared" which means the tears are answered with shared food. In the other six scenes the person in tears is hurried along or suspected or scolded. A woman who is "weeping bitterly" in prayer is accused of being drunk. A husband who follows his wife "weeping as he went" is told "Go! Return!" by an officer. A king who weeps for a dead son is told to get up and face his men or else lose the men by nightfall.

The woman who was accused of being drunk gives the clearest picture of what being heard does. She answers "I poured out my soul" and the priest changes his tone and wishes her peace and the text says that she "went her way and ate" and that her face was no longer sad.

## What the finding could mean

One line in the book of Ecclesiastes looks at the tears of people under oppression and says "they had no comforter". The count on this page fits that line because the largest single group of scenes is the group where tears get no recorded response.

Sadness that is seen and asked about is a road to being known and being known by another heart is one of the three basic human needs beside food and shelter. The record shows the question about the trouble only six times and in most of those six scenes the question leads straight to help such as water or a rescue or a name spoken aloud.

The record offers two things a person can do. A person who is near someone in tears can use the rare response and ask "What troubles you?" and then wait for the answer because five of the six people who were asked in these stories got to say what was wrong. The question lands best with a reason and an offer such as "I saw that you were crying and I have half an hour to listen" and with water or food beside the question if the asker has some to spare. A person who is in tears can let the tears be seen and say what the tears are about because the man who hid his tears twice was answered after he wept aloud. Showing the tears is no guarantee because ten of the 61 scenes end with the person told to stop and thirteen end with no response at all.

## Two quick points

- In 61 scenes of weeping in the stories of the World English Bible the largest group has no recorded response and being asked what the trouble is happens in 6 scenes out of 61.
- A person near someone in tears can ask "What troubles you?" and wait for the answer and a person in tears can say aloud what the tears are about because the scenes where tears are seen and asked about are the scenes that lead to help.
