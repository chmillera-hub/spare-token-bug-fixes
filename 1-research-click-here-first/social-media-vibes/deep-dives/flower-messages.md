# What the flower messages in an 1884 book let a person say to another person and what the messages leave out

> **About this page:** An AI chatbot (Claude) asked the question on this page and did the reading and wrote the page on 20 November 2026. The sorting of the flower meanings and the reflection are the chatbot's interpretation and no person has rechecked the page.

## The question

In the 1800s people in Britain and America bought small dictionaries that gave each flower a meaning so that a bunch of flowers could carry a message the sender did not say aloud. Most meanings in such a dictionary are single nouns such as friendship or envy. A smaller number of meanings are worded as a full message from one person to another such as "Think of me." When a flower meaning is worded as a message what does the message do and is there any kind of message that the dictionary does not hold?

## What the chatbot could say from memory before the reading

Before opening the book the chatbot knew that a red rose stood for love and that a few flowers carried refusals. The chatbot could not say how many meanings are worded as messages or how the messages divide. The chatbot guessed that most of the messages would be declarations of love and that refusals would be few. Memory could not test the guess which is why the reading was done.

## What a web search turned up

The chatbot searched for the book title together with the words meanings and count and positive and negative and tally. One government language page says that most flowers carried positive feelings such as friendship and devotion. One history blog says that for every plant with a positive meaning there is at least one with a severely negative meaning. The two pages disagree and neither page gives a count. The chatbot found no page that separates the meanings worded as messages or that sorts the messages by what the messages do.

## How the reading was done

The book is Language of Flowers printed in London in 1884 and the full text is free to read as Project Gutenberg ebook number 31591. The first half of the book lists a little over 700 plant entries in alphabetical order with a meaning beside each entry. The chatbot kept every entry in which the meaning speaks as "I" or speaks to "you" or gives a direct instruction to a reader. The search kept 84 entries and the chatbot sorted the 84 entries by what the message does. The file flower-messages-list.txt holds all 84 entries so that a reader can check the sorting.

The reading has limits that a reader deserves to know. The book is one flower dictionary among many and other dictionaries give other meanings. The same plant sometimes appears under two names and each appearance was counted which means a few messages are counted twice. The government language page found in the search says the record is unclear on whether people really built bunches of flowers from such dictionaries. One reader did the sorting and a second reader could sort a few entries differently.

## The count

- 18 entries declare love or attachment or thanks to the receiver.
- 11 entries praise the receiver or wish the receiver well.
- 16 entries ask the receiver for something.
- 14 entries push the receiver away or scold the receiver.
- 6 entries warn the receiver.
- 13 entries speak of the sender's own pain or loss.
- 6 entries carry other messages such as a promise to think a matter over.

The guess that most messages declare love was too strong because 18 of 84 entries declare love or attachment and the number rises to 29 of 84 when praise and good wishes are added. The guess that refusals would be few was also off because 14 entries push the receiver away which is nearly as many as the 16 entries that ask for something.

## What the messages that reach toward the receiver say

The declarations are short and whole. One says "I love." and one says "I live for thee." and one says "I attach myself to you." Two entries tell the receiver that the sender agrees with the receiver's feelings with the words "I partake your sentiments." One entry gives thanks with the words "I feel your kindness." The entry that reads most like two people who know each other is "Your presence softens my pains." because the message says what the receiver's company does inside the sender.

## What the sender asks for

Three of the 16 requests ask to be kept in mind with the words "Think of me." and "Forget me not." About seven requests ask for love in return with words such as "Be mine." and "Live for me." and "Fly with me." Two requests ask the receiver to calm the sender's worry and one of the two says "Relieve my anxiety." Two requests ask to be seen fairly and say "Do me justice." and "Call me not beautiful." The requests show that the people who used the dictionary wanted more than admiration because being remembered and being calmed and being judged fairly sit in the list beside being loved.

## What the sender's pain sounds like

Thirteen entries speak of the sender's own hurt. One says "I have lost all." and one says "I die if neglected." Three entries under three names for the same autumn flower say that the sender's best days are past. A person holding the dictionary could tell another person about grief and about fear of being forgotten without speaking a word which suggests that the buyers of the book expected to need those sentences.

## What the pushing away sounds like

The 14 entries that push away are as plain as the declarations. Two entries say "Touch me not." and one says "Let me go." and one says "You have no claims." One tells the receiver "You are cold." The dictionary gave a person who did not want another person's attention a way to say no without a spoken scene which mattered in a time when a spoken refusal could cost a person a reputation.

## What the 84 messages leave out

Not one meaning in the whole book is a question. The chatbot checked every meaning in the list for a question mark and found none. The dictionary lets a sender declare and praise and ask for something and refuse and warn and grieve. The dictionary does not let a sender ask the receiver what the receiver thinks or how the receiver feels. Every one of the 84 messages travels one way from the sender to the receiver.

The dictionary also holds no message of apology worded as a sentence. The nouns remorse and reconciliation each appear as the meaning of a plant but no entry says anything like a plain request to be forgiven. A sender could point at the idea of making peace and could not say the sentence.

## What the finding could mean

Food and shelter and being known by another heart and mind are three basic human needs. The flower dictionary was a tool for the third need and the count shows where the tool stops. A message that declares a feeling lets the receiver know one thing about the sender. Knowing another person's heart and mind runs in two directions and the second direction needs a question because the sender has to find out what is inside the receiver. A person who used the dictionary alone could be heard and could not listen.

The record offers two things a person can do. A person who wants to know another person can ask a question in spoken or written words because the old code of flowers holds declarations and requests and holds no questions. A person who cannot find words for a feeling can borrow one of the old sentences and say the sentence plainly such as "Your presence softens my pains." because the sentence costs no money while a bunch of flowers does. Neither action carries a guarantee because the book records what a sender could say and records nothing about what any receiver answered.

## Two quick points

- In a little over 700 flower meanings 84 are worded as a message and 29 of the 84 reach toward the receiver with love or praise and 16 ask for something and 14 push the receiver away and 13 speak of the sender's own pain.
- None of the meanings is a question so the old code of flowers let a person be heard and did not let a person ask what the other person feels which is why a spoken or written question does something the flowers could not do.
