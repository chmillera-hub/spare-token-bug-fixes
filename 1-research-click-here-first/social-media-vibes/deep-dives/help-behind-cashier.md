# Help behind a cashier: when asking for help means buying a service

> **About this page:** This page holds two reflections on the advice to "try therapy" or "see a doctor" given to a person who says "I am struggling." An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote the first reflection on 20 November 2026 and no other person has rechecked the reflection. The author of this repository supplied the second reflection and the text appears in full.

## Reflection one

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

"You can ask for help" often turns out to mean "you can buy help" because the two doors people point to which are therapy and medical care both have a cashier standing in front of the door.

**The advice that costs the adviser nothing**

A person says "I am struggling" and the listener answers "have you tried therapy?" or "see a doctor." The listener has now finished the conversation in one sentence and spent no time and no money. The struggling person has been handed a bill. A therapy session can cost a hundred dollars or more for under an hour and the arrangement forbids the therapist from being part of the person's life outside the paid hour. A medical visit can lead to tests and prescriptions and follow up visits and each one arrives with a separate charge that the person often cannot see in advance.

**How the help can become the injury**

A person who was already short on money and follows the advice can end up owing thousands. Debt collectors start calling. A court can order money taken straight out of a paycheck. Rent goes unpaid because the money went to the clinic and unpaid rent ends with armed deputies removing the person from the home against the person's will. So a person who went looking for healing can finish the journey sicker from stress and without a place to sleep. Some treatment does heal and some professionals care deeply and the financial risk is still real enough that anyone recommending the path owes the listener a plain statement of the risk.

**The vanishing adviser**

The harshest part is what happens next. The same people who said "get help" tend to go quiet when the help produces ruin. Here is a possibility about why: admitting that the advice led to bankruptcy would mean admitting that the advice was given without knowing the cost. So the adviser steps back and the ruined person hears a new sentence which is "have you thought about picking up more hours?" The person is now told to work more in order to pay for the help that was supposed to address the exhaustion from working. Each piece of advice sends the person to another cashier and none of the advisers stays.

**What the referral is doing in the conversation**

"Try therapy" can work as an exit for the speaker. The sentence sounds caring and the sentence moves the struggling person out of the speaker's hands and into a paid service. The speaker gets to feel helpful without sitting in the discomfort of another person's pain for even ten minutes. A referral given that way is a polite form of "not my concern" and the listener can feel the difference in the body as a door closing.

**What was actually being asked for**

In many cases the person who said "I am struggling" was asking for a human being and not for a service. The need underneath was to be heard for an hour by someone who would still be around next week and who would not send an invoice. That kind of presence is exactly what the paid arrangement cannot supply because the paid arrangement ends when the clock runs out and the payment stops.

**What a caring answer sounds like**

A caring answer states what the speaker can give such as "I have an hour tonight and I want to hear what is going on because I care what happens to you." A person with no hour to give can say that plainly too. If the speaker does mention professional help a caring version includes the risks such as the cost per session and the chance of surprise bills so the listener can decide with full information. The difference between the two kinds of answer is the difference between handing a hungry person a restaurant menu and sitting down to share a meal.

## Reflection two

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied this reflection on 20 November 2026 and the text appears in full.

Asking for help has been quietly redefined as hiring a vendor.

When society tells you it is completely fine to reach out, what it almost always means is that you are allowed to pay a licensed professional by the hour so nobody in your actual life has to be inconvenienced. People toss out suggestions to see a therapist or go to a doctor as if those are free public fountains, completely glossing over the fact that both sit behind steep paywalls. Even when a provider genuinely wants to help, the relationship is still wired into a billing system—the moment your insurance balks or your bank account hits zero, the care shuts off.

What makes that advice feel so rigged is that the people handing it out have zero skin in the game. Telling someone to call a professional lets the advice-giver walk away feeling helpful and responsible without ever having to sit in the mud with you, listen for an hour, or share any real weight. They offload the emotional labor onto a paid clinic and offload the entire financial risk straight onto your back.

Then comes the coldest part of the loop. If following their advice actually drains your savings or buries you in medical debt, those exact same people rarely stick around to acknowledge the wreckage. Financial ruin makes people deeply uncomfortable, because it reminds them how thin the ice is under their own feet. So they quietly ghost you, avoid the awkwardness, and if they say anything at all, they hand you the ultimate punchline: suggesting you just grind out more hours at work to pay off the bills you racked up trying to heal from the grind in the first place.

It turns basic human care into a luxury product—and then treats you like a cautionary tale if buying it wipes you out.

Do you think most people who push that advice genuinely forget how fast medical and therapy bills can ruin someone, or do they just not care as long as it gets an uncomfortable conversation off their plate?
