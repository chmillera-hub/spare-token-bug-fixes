# Fewer babies each year: what the numbers say and what the numbers are made of

> **About this page:** This page holds three reflections on the falling number of births in the United States. An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote the first two reflections on 21 November 2026 after looking up the figures and no other person has rechecked the figures. The author of this repository supplied the third reflection.

## Where the numbers are from

The shares of women who have not had a baby are from the Current Population Survey Fertility Supplement for June 2024 as reported by the United States Census Bureau at https://census.gov/library/stories/2025/09/older-mothers.html. The birth figures for 2025 are provisional figures from the National Center for Health Statistics at https://cdc.gov/nchs/pressroom/releases/20260409.html. The figure of 1.57 lifetime births per woman is from news coverage of the same provisional report.

## Reflection one: the numbers

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

**The share of women with no baby yet in 2024**

- **Ages 20 to 24.** The figure is 85 percent which is ten points above the 75 percent of 2014.
- **Ages 25 to 29.** The figure is 63 percent and the figure was 49.6 percent a decade earlier. A thirteen point jump in ten years is enormous for a number like that.
- **Ages 30 to 34.** The figure is 40.3 percent and the figure was 28.9 percent ten years before.

**The overall rate**

Lifetime births per woman sank to 1.57 in 2025 which is the lowest ever recorded and far under the 2.1 needed to hold a population steady. About 3.6 million babies arrived in 2025 which is one percent fewer than the year before. Since 2007 the yearly rate of births per thousand women aged 15 to 44 has dropped almost 23 percent. At 1.57 each generation is about three quarters the size of the one before unless people move in from elsewhere.

**Where the drop is happening**

In a single year the birth rate for women aged 25 to 29 slid about 4.4 percent while the rate for women aged 30 to 34 crept up about 2.7 percent. Gains among women in their thirties and forties have been too small to make up for the losses among women under thirty. So the story that "women are just having babies later" covers only part of the hole.

**The one number that has not moved yet**

Among women aged 45 to 50 the share with no baby went down from 16.7 percent to 14.9 percent over the decade. One researcher quoted in the news coverage says the delay has so far not produced a notable rise in women finishing the usual years for having a baby without one. That number offers little comfort. The women who are 45 to 50 today were in their twenties two decades ago when housing cost less and far fewer were without a baby at 28. Nobody has ever watched a group that was 63 percent without a baby at 25 to 29 and 40 percent without a baby at 30 to 34 reach the end of the window. Catching up from that far behind would require a surge of births in the late thirties that has never happened at that scale. A best guess is that the late life figure starts climbing within ten years.

**What is driving the numbers**

The Census Bureau's own explanation is that people in their twenties are putting money security ahead of starting a family because of the rising weight of housing and food and gas costs. That is a government office saying in plain words that people cannot afford shelter and groceries and a baby at the same time.

## Reflection two: what the missing babies are made of

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

A country can run out of babies without a single law against babies because rent and loneliness do the work that no law would dare to do.

**What a ten point jump in ten years means**

Numbers like these usually creep. A share that moves thirteen points in a decade among women in their late twenties is a stampede. Something happened to an entire generation between 2014 and 2024 and the something was not a sudden dislike of babies. Rents climbed. Wages lagged. Work swallowed evenings. Partners became scarce because people stopped meeting each other anywhere that did not charge admission.

**The official explanation says the quiet part**

The Census Bureau's own write up says that today's twentysomethings rank money security ahead of starting a family because housing and food and gas weigh more each year. Read that as a plain sentence. A government office is reporting that adults in the richest country on Earth look at the price of shelter and groceries and conclude that a baby would sink the household. The people who set those prices and those wages have made a new human being into a luxury purchase.

**The comforting story and the hole in the story**

The comforting story is "women are simply waiting and will have babies later." Part of that is true because births to women in their thirties and forties have been rising for a decade. The rise has been too small to cover the fall among women under thirty. Part of the drop is also teenagers having far fewer babies which many people welcome. Neither fact fills the hole. A woman who starts at 34 has fewer years and a body that makes pregnancy less certain. A plan that depends on millions of women all succeeding in a narrow late window is a plan built on luck.

**The number people hide behind**

Women aged 45 to 50 are the single group whose share with no baby went down over the decade. People wave that number around as proof that everything works out in the end. Those women were in their twenties in the early 2000s when a starter home was within reach. Nobody has ever seen a generation that was 63 percent without a baby in the late twenties arrive at 45. That experiment is running right now on living people and the results will be in when the chance to change the results has passed.

**What the missing babies are made of**

Every missing baby is a decision that some couple or some single person made at a kitchen table. "The landlord raised the rent again." "My job could end any month and then armed deputies put a family on the curb." "I have not been on a date in three years." "Who would help me at three in the morning?" Surveys keep finding that people want more babies than they end up having. So the falling number is a tally of wishes that died in the gap between wanting a family and being able to house and feed one.

**Who gains from the arrangement**

A worker with no family to raise can be worked longer and moved more easily. A person alone buys more distractions. People who hold large amounts of property profit when housing is scarce and dear. Here is a possibility that deserves to be said out loud about the people with the most money and power: the conditions that are strangling family formation are the same conditions that are enriching them and so they have little reason to fix anything. Those same people then give speeches about the birth rate while changing nothing that would let a couple in their twenties afford two bedrooms.

**What shrinking looks like on the ground**

Shrinking does not arrive as a crash. Shrinking arrives as a school that closes and a street with fewer strollers each year and a town where the youngest regular at the diner is fifty. Fewer workers end up supporting more elders. The elders have fewer relatives to sit with them. The loneliness that helped cause the drop returns as old age with nobody at the bedside.

**What would actually move the number**

Speeches and one time cash gifts have barely moved the number in any country that tried them. What a person needs before bringing a baby into the world is a home that cannot be taken and enough hours free of work to raise the baby and other adults close by who will help. That means housing priced within a single wage and work weeks short enough to leave a life and villages where raising the next generation is shared among many hands. Those are choices for people with office and wealth. Until those people make different choices each year's report will show the same thing which is fewer babies and another record low and another round of officials acting surprised.

## Reflection three

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied this reflection on 21 November 2026.

Any biologist can tell you that when an environment becomes unstable, scarce, and isolating, populations naturally stop growing. Nobody looks at a shifting ecosystem and lectures the wildlife for having a bad attitude. Yet when young adults respond to a strained, high-pressure environment in the exact same rational way, politicians, economists, and commentators act like they are staring at an unsolvable cultural mystery.

What we are watching is an economic model quietly undercutting its own foundation. The entire system depends on a steady next generation of workers, taxpayers, and neighbors to keep functioning, yet it has systematically priced out the basic conditions required to raise them. Everyday housing has been turned into a speculative asset class, two full-time incomes often barely cover baseline stability, and the cost of care for the next generation rivals a second mortgage. It is a self-defeating loop—optimizing everything for short-term returns while hollowing out the long-term future, then acting bewildered when the numbers drop.

Even before anyone sits down to do the math on a mortgage or daycare, the basic path to finding a partner has been completely hollowed out. Nearly every free, casual, everyday place where people used to naturally cross paths has vanished, and in their place we handed human connection over to publicly traded tech companies. Look at the sheer absurdity of that business model: the primary infrastructure for modern dating is run by apps whose revenue actually drops the moment two people fall in love and delete their accounts. We essentially outsourced the continuation of society to engagement algorithms designed to keep people scrolling.

Then come the official solutions, which only highlight how out of touch the conversation has become. Whenever demographic projections look grim, the default response is a modest one-time tax credit, a small cash bonus, or a wave of lectures telling twenty-somethings to prioritize family values. You cannot nudge someone into a twenty-year responsibility with a minor rebate when the core pillars—affordable shelter, manageable work hours, and a reliable local network of support—are missing. Offering a small check while leaving the underlying math completely broken misses the entire point.

The most frustrating part is how easily the blame gets shifted onto the people navigating this mess. Young adults are routinely painted as stunted, overly picky, or allergic to commitment, when in reality many of them are making a clear-eyed, deeply responsible judgment call. Hesitating to bring the next generation into precarious housing, nonstop burnout, and thin social support is not a sign of a broken generation—it is a sign of people taking the well-being of the next generation seriously. The birth rate is not falling because people stopped wanting love or families; it is falling because we built an economy optimized for quarterly output rather than sustainable human life, and reality is finally catching up to the math.
