# A request to rename a sport read as a loyalty test: two reflections

This page holds two reflections about one social media post. The post said that a president wishes the country's biggest professional football league to change its name so that soccer is the lone sport called football. Nobody checked whether the quote in the post is real. The owner of this repository suggested that the request could be a loyalty test which means a way to see which followers will change something that matters to them. The anonymized post and comments sit in the page named league-rename-thread.md one folder up. Both reflections treat the loyalty test as one possible reading because nobody can see inside another person's mind and the page takes no side.

## Reflection one

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection on 15 November 2026 and no other person has rechecked the reflection.

The loyalty test reading fits a known way that powerful people sort followers because a cheap "yes" proves nothing and a costly "yes" proves a lot. Asking football fans to give up the word "football" would be a costly ask since the word is tied to Sunday afternoons with relatives and decades of habit. A follower who gives up something loved shows skin in the game and a follower who only nods along gets exposed.

Several commenters under the post noticed the cost without calling the cost a test because the commenters wrote that the request would irritate the president's own supporters who love saying "this is real football." Under the loyalty test reading that irritation is the point and not a mistake since a test that costs nothing sorts nobody.

The loyalty test is one possibility and the commenters offered others such as an old grudge against the league and a hunger for legacy and a way to pull attention from grocery prices. The loyalty test reading and the attention reading can both hold at once because a loyalty test also fills the news while rent goes undiscussed.

The part that matters for an ordinary person is the same under every reading because a person can decline to spend energy on the name of a sport and keep saying "I am short on rent this month" until somebody with power answers that sentence.

## Reflection two

That makes total sense—it is not just passive flattery he is testing for, it is costly signaling.

Anyone can cheer at a rally, wear a hat, or nod along when the leader says something that already matches what they believe. Cheap agreement costs nothing, which means a leader surrounded by yes-people never actually knows who is genuinely loyal and who is just riding the wave until it becomes inconvenient.

To filter out cheap yes-people, you demand a sacrifice that actually hurts: change something deeply ingrained in your own daily life and cultural identity just because I asked.

- Separating Cheap Applause from Real Devotion: Saying "you're doing a great job" takes zero effort. Giving up the word football—a term wired into your regional pride, your family traditions, and how you talk every Sunday—forces a real internal friction. If someone actually makes that switch, they have proved they value the leader's word more than their own habits.
- The Power of Voluntary Cost: In social psychology, a loyalty test only works if the target has to pay a visible social price in front of their own peers. If you start calling gridiron football Super Ball or Handegg at a backyard barbecue, your friends are going to look at you like you lost your mind. Willingness to absorb that embarrassment is the exact proof of skin in the game the test is designed to measure.
- Why This Specific Test Backfires: The catch is that a loyalty test only works when the crowd believes the leader is asking them to sacrifice for a shared cause or survival. When the demand is just renaming a sport on a whim while everyday life stays hard, even dedicated supporters look at the price tag and decide it is a bridge too far.

Instead of exposing who is truly loyal, a test that arbitrary just reveals where the crowd's actual floor is.
