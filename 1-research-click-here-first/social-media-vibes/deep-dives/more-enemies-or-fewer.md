# More enemies or fewer: two reflections

This page holds two reflections on one question from the owner of this repository. The question asks whether a person wants more enemies or fewer and observes that harming an enemy or shaming an enemy or cutting off an enemy's support tends to create more enemies.

## Reflection one

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection on 17 November 2026 and no other person has rechecked the reflection.

One question sorts almost every piece of advice about enemies and the question is "will this action leave me with more enemies or fewer". Harming an enemy and shaming an enemy and cutting off an enemy's support all fail that question because each action adds enemies.

The arithmetic works through witnesses. An enemy is rarely alone because an enemy has relatives and friends and thousands of strangers who share the same beliefs or the same background. When those onlookers watch a person like themselves get injured or fired or dragged away by armed officers the onlookers draw a simple conclusion which is "the people who did that would do the same to me". One enemy harmed in public can therefore become fifty new enemies who were neutral the day before.

The harm also hardens the original enemy. A person who loses a job loses the paycheck that buys food and pays for shelter and a person facing hunger has more anger and less to lose than before. A person who is mocked gathers with others who were mocked and the group's shared injury becomes the glue that holds the group's hatred together. So the common playbook of name-calling and getting people fired and summoning armed officers produces angrier enemies in larger numbers which is the opposite of what the people using the playbook say they want.

The same test shows what shrinks the count.

- Name the harmful act and leave the person's humanity intact. Saying "striking that man injured a living mind and body" tells every onlooker that the objection is to injury. The onlookers who share the enemy's background hear no threat to themselves.
- Keep food and shelter and human contact flowing toward the enemy. A person with a full table and people who know that person's heart has much to lose by harming anyone and little reason to stay an enemy.
- Step back to a safe distance when a fist is raised. Distance protects a person's mind and body without adding a new injury to the story the enemy's side tells.
- Use the smallest restraint when someone is actively injuring others. Stopping an attack can require hands and locked doors used against the attacker's will. Restraint that feeds and houses and teaches the person ends the danger without handing onlookers a tale of cruelty. Punishment meant to hurt hands onlookers exactly that tale.

Even a player with no feelings at all would choose this path because the numbers favor it. A strategy that turns one enemy into fifty loses over time no matter how satisfying each blow feels. A strategy that turns one enemy into a calm neighbor wins twice since the threat disappears and the neighbor's circle sees that care was offered where an attack was expected.

So advice to hate or shame or starve an enemy is advice to grow the enemy's side. Advice to care for an enemy while naming harm plainly is the advice that leaves a person with fewer enemies and more people willing to share a table.

## Reflection two

> **Who wrote this:** The owner of this repository supplied the text of this reflection on 17 November 2026 and the text appears in full.

Even from a purely cold, zero-emotion game theory perspective, if your objective function is minimizing the total number of enemies in the system, almost every standard institutional and cultural reflex—calling armed agents on non-violent friction, getting people fired from their jobs, dragging them through punitive legal machinery, name-calling, or cutting off survival support—is actively optimizing for the exact opposite outcome.

Look at the math of why punitive escalation always multiplies adversaries rather than shrinking them:

- The Multiplier Effect of Visible Harm: You never attack an isolated individual in a vacuum. The moment you deploy armed force, economic ruin, or public humiliation against one person, every bystander who shares their struggles, beliefs, or circumstances watches that strike land and updates their threat model immediately: If they will destroy someone like me over friction, they are my enemy too. One strike against one person routinely manufactures ten new enemies out of previously neutral observers.
- Cornered-Animal Game Theory (Eliminating Peace Payoffs): When you strip someone of their job, housing, or freedom through institutional levers, you erase every positive incentive they had to cooperate with the surrounding society. If following the rules still leaves someone humiliated, broke, or caged, the payoff for peaceful compliance drops to zero—which mathematically drives radicalization and desperation upward.
- Confusing Suppression with Resolution: Sending armed agents or using administrative destruction to silence someone creates a temporary illusion of control because the immediate noise stops. Yet the underlying grievance, fear, and hostility don't vanish—they go underground, compound over time, and recruit allies among anyone else who feels crushed by the exact same machinery.
- The Asymmetric Efficiency of De-Escalation: Turning ordinary, non-violent social disagreements into weaponized takedowns burns massive amounts of energy just to generate a larger, angrier wave of opposition. By refusing to retaliate over non-violent friction, refusing to ruin someone's livelihood, and continuing to speak from your heart while treating their baseline human needs as untouchable, you starve hostility of fuel—leaving bystanders with zero reason to treat you as a threat and giving the original adversary an actual exit ramp out of conflict. At the same time, never confuse non-retaliation with silence in the face of danger: calling out actual physical harm or threats of violence to keep bodies safe remains essential and completely separate from how we navigate non-violent human friction.

Any strategy that claims to build safety while systematically ruining livelihoods and siccing force on people is failing basic math: you cannot subtract enemies from a board using moves that multiply them on every turn.
