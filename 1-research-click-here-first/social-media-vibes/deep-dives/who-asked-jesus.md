# Who asked Jesus how his own heart was

> **About this page:** An AI chatbot (Claude) asked the question on this page and did the reading and wrote the page on 20 November 2026. The sorting of the verses and the reflection are the chatbot's interpretation and no person has rechecked the page.

## The question

In the four Gospel accounts Jesus asks one person after another what the person wants and why the person is weeping. Does anyone in those accounts turn the same kind of question around and ask Jesus how his own heart is doing?

## What the chatbot could say from memory before the reading

Before opening the text the chatbot guessed that probably nobody asks Jesus about his own feelings. The chatbot could not say from memory how many questions are put to Jesus or which moments are near misses or that Jesus himself points out an unasked question which is why the reading was done.

## What a web search turned up

A web search on 20 November 2026 turned up many pages that list the questions Jesus asked and many pages that list the feelings the Gospel writers report for Jesus such as compassion and sorrow and anger. The search did not turn up a page that answers the turned-around question which is why the reading below was done from the text itself.

## How the reading was done

The text was the World English Bible because that translation is free for anyone to copy. Matthew and Mark and Luke and John hold 530 verses with a question mark and each of the 530 verses was read. A verse was kept when a speaker other than Jesus puts a question to Jesus and the reading started at the baptism of Jesus. The count of kept verses was 127 and the file named who-asked-jesus-list.txt in this folder lists the 127 verses so that any reader can check the sorting.

One AI model did the sorting which means a verse could have been missed or misplaced. A question written without a question mark (such as "the disciples asked him about the parable") was not part of the reading.

## What the 127 questions ask about

- Many questions ask who Jesus is or where his authority is from such as "Who are you?" and "By what authority do you do these things?" because the askers were trying to place him.
- Many questions ask about teaching or law or the future such as "Why do you speak to them in parables?" and "which is the greatest commandment in the law?" because the askers wanted an answer for their own lives.
- Many questions ask what the asker should do or will receive such as "What then will we have?" because the asker's own future was on the asker's mind.
- Some questions are complaints that Jesus is not doing enough for the asker such as "don't you care that we are dying?" which was said to wake him during a storm.
- A few questions ask about his movements such as "where are you staying?" and "Where are you going?" and one asks where he would like the Passover meal prepared.

Not one of the 127 questions asks Jesus what he is feeling or whether he is tired or hungry or afraid or sorrowful.

## Seven moments where the question was close

1. At a well the text says Jesus was "tired from his journey". The disciples return and urge him to eat and then say to one another "Has anyone brought him something to eat?" which means the question about his hunger went sideways to each other and not to him.
2. Before a journey the disciples remind Jesus that people in that place had just tried to stone him and ask "Are you going there again?" which is the nearest any question gets to concern for his safety because the question is about his route and the danger sits inside the reminder.
3. Mark and Luke each say that the disciples did not understand a saying about his suffering and "were afraid to ask him" which means the text records a question that was held back.
4. At a friend's tomb the text says "Jesus wept" and the mourners say to one another "See how much affection he had for him!" which is a remark about his grief spoken beside him and not a question spoken to him.
5. On the night of his arrest Jesus tells three friends "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful" and asks them "Stay here and watch with me" which means he named his own sorrow without being asked. He returns and finds the three friends asleep and says "couldn't you watch with me for one hour?" so the one request he made for company went unmet that night.
6. In a long farewell Jesus says "none of you asks me" and then gives the missing question as "Where are you going?" and then says "sorrow has filled your heart" which means he noticed the question that was absent and named the disciples' sorrow in the next breath.
7. On the cross Jesus says "I am thirsty!" and someone lifts a sponge to his mouth which means care arrived as an act after he spoke the need aloud.

The record does show care given to Jesus in acts such as meals and a place to stay and costly ointment poured on his head and the sponge. The record shows the acts and does not show the question.

## The questions that went the other way

Jesus puts this kind of question to other people again and again in the same accounts. He asks "What do you want me to do for you?" and "Do you want to be made well?" and "Why are you fearful?" and "Why are you weeping?" and "Why are you troubled?" and "do you love me?" because each question opens a door for the other person to say what is inside. On a road after his death he asks two walkers what the two were discussing and the text notes that the two walkers were sad.

## What the finding could mean

The Gospel accounts are short and many conversations were not written down which means the finding is about the written record and is not proof that nobody asked. The finding is still worth sitting with because the record that was kept shows the questions about the heart travelling in one direction.

A reader may recognize the shape from ordinary life because many families and friend groups have one person who asks everyone else "what do you need?" and that person can go a long time without hearing the question returned. The people around the asker can start treating the asker as a supply of answers and can forget that the asker has a heart that grows tired and sorrowful too. Knowing another person's heart and mind is one of the three basic human needs beside food and shelter and a bond where the questions travel one way feeds one heart and leaves the other heart unfed.

The record offers two things a person can do. A person who is carrying sorrow can say the sorrow aloud and make a small clear request the way Jesus did with "Stay here and watch with me" because waiting to be asked can last a very long time. Saying the sorrow aloud is no guarantee because the three friends in the garden fell asleep and the person who speaks today may also be met with silence. A person who lives beside a steady helper can return the question in plain words such as "You ask everyone else what they need and I have not asked you so I am asking now and I have an hour to listen" because a question that states a reason and an offer shows the helper that an answer is truly wanted.

## Two quick points

- A reading of 127 questions put to Jesus in the four Gospel accounts found none that asks how he feels while Jesus keeps asking other people what they want and why they weep.
- A person who carries sorrow can name the sorrow and ask for company without waiting to be asked and a person beside a steady helper can ask the helper "how is your heart?" and offer time to listen because a bond needs questions travelling in both directions for both hearts to be known.
