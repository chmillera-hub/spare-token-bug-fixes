# The mind as a wall of locks and as one large enzyme: two reflections on why most incoming information is ignored

> **About this page:** This page holds two reflections on an idea that the author of this repository proposed: the mind holds a limited store of patterns that are each tied to a behavior and incoming information matters only when a piece of that information fits one of the stored patterns. An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote both reflections on 25 November 2026 and no other person has rechecked the reflections. The reflections are a way of picturing the mind and are not measured brain science.

## Reflection one: the amount of data matters far less than whether any piece fits a stored pattern

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

### The idea in one picture

The senses pour in a flood and the mind acts like a wall of locks. Each lock is a stored pattern tied to a behavior such as "food so eat" or "sudden loud noise so flinch." A billion pieces of data can wash past the wall and the pieces shaped like a key are the ones that turn anything. Everything else drains away because no lock exists for the rest. A person looking at a plate of food can have endless detail in the background and the mind ignores the background because none of the detail matches a behavior such as "scary thing so run."

### Where the idea fits what is known

As far as the chatbot knows the eyes and ears and skin deliver something like millions of bits each second while conscious attention handles a few dozen bits each second. So the mind must be discarding nearly everything and the discarding has to follow some rule. "Keep what matches a stored pattern" is a strong candidate for that rule since a creature that had to weigh every leaf in the background would be eaten before finishing.

### Four refinements that make the idea stronger

- **Matching happens in degrees.** A blurry shape that is partly like a snake still triggers a jump because a partial match can be enough when the stored behavior protects survival. A sharper image gives a closer match and a stronger signal.
- **Patterns compete.** Several patterns can match at once such as food on the table and a stranger at the door. The strongest match wins control of behavior and the others are held back which is why a frightened person can forget a meal sitting right in front of them.
- **The state of mind and body changes the locks.** A person who has not eaten all day has a loosened food lock so faint smells and small pictures of food trigger the lock. The same data produces different behavior depending on which patterns are primed by current needs.
- **"Nothing matches" can itself be a pattern.** Something truly strange often triggers a stop and stare response. That response is how new patterns get built which means the store is not fixed. Learning adds locks and a new lock makes a whole class of old background data suddenly visible.

### What follows from the idea

- **More information does not change a person unless a pattern exists to catch the information.** A speaker can pour detailed truth at a listener and if the listener has no stored behavior that the truth connects to then the words pass through like background leaves. That would explain the smile and nod that greets a complex idea.
- **Teaching works by attaching new data to an existing lock.** An idea about housing lands when the idea is tied to something the listener already acts on such as fear of eviction or love for a baby. A message shaped like an existing key gets through where an abstract message drains away.
- **People who want attention build keys on purpose.** An advertisement uses a crisp picture of food or money or a smiling face because those shapes fit locks nearly everyone carries. The background of the advertisement barely matters.
- **A person can live beside something important for years and never see the important thing.** A lonely neighbor or a harmful arrangement stays invisible to anyone with no pattern for noticing. Giving people the pattern such as a short checklist or a named idea can make the invisible thing jump out.

### One place to hold the idea loosely

A fixed count such as 10000 patterns is a stand-in since stored patterns blend and combine and nobody has a way to count them. The mind also seems to build new responses on the spot by joining old ones. So the store behaves more like a set of building pieces than a finite list although the core claim survives: data with nothing to connect to is ignored.

## Reflection two: the mind pictured as one very complicated enzyme

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

The author of this repository then proposed a second picture. The mind is one very complicated enzyme. An enzyme is a large molecule with pockets and each pocket fits one kind of smaller molecule which is called a substrate. In this picture the signals from the senses are the substrates and each pocket is a stored pattern. The enzyme picture adds something the lock picture lacked because what binds at one pocket can change what happens at another pocket.

### How the pieces line up

- **Sense data as substrate.** A sight or sound or smell arrives and drifts past the mind. Most of the arriving data has no pocket to fit so the data floats away unused which is the background being ignored.
- **Stored patterns as binding pockets.** Each pocket is shaped for one kind of input such as the look of food or the shape of a threat. A close fit binds tightly and a rough fit binds weakly which matches the idea that a sharper image triggers a stronger response.
- **Behavior as the product.** An enzyme with the right substrate in place produces something. A mind with the right input in place produces an action such as reaching or fleeing.

### Where the enzyme picture goes further

- **Two substrates and one combined product.** Many enzymes need two different molecules bound at once before any reaction happens. The author's example is that case: the food pocket is filled and the danger pocket is filled and the product is a single joined action of grabbing the food and running. Neither input alone would produce that action.
- **One site changing another site.** In an enzyme a molecule that binds at a side pocket can reshape the main pocket which either helps or blocks the main reaction. Fear works that way on appetite: a strong threat signal reshapes the whole mind so that the food pocket stops responding. A feeling of safety does the reverse and lets the wish for food register again.
- **Competition for the same pocket.** Two similar molecules can fight over one pocket and the one that fits better or arrives in greater amount wins. A bright advertisement and a quiet face across the table can compete for the same attention pocket and the louder and more frequent input tends to win.
- **Saturation.** An enzyme has a top speed and extra substrate beyond that point changes nothing. A mind flooded with a thousand alerts responds no faster than a mind given ten which supports the point that the size of the flood matters little once the matching pockets are full.
- **More or fewer pockets over time.** Cells build more of an enzyme when a substrate keeps showing up and fewer when the substrate is absent. Practice does the same to a mind: a pattern that is used daily gets stronger and faster and a pattern that is never used fades.

### Where the picture stretches

- **The shape of an enzyme is mostly fixed and the shape of a mind is not.** Genes set the pockets of an enzyme. A mind grows brand new pockets through learning so a person can gain a response to a word or a symbol that meant nothing a year earlier. The mind is closer to an enzyme that keeps redesigning its own pockets based on what has bound before.
- **A mind supplies its own substrate.** Memories and imagined scenes can fill the pockets with no outside input at all. A person lying in a dark quiet room can still trigger the fear response by picturing a threat.
- **Time order matters.** An enzyme mostly responds to what is bound right now. A mind responds to sequence since "food and then danger" can lead to a different action than "danger and then food."

### What the picture is useful for

The enzyme view explains why a message can fail even when every word is true. A message lands when the message fits a pocket the listener already has and the message lands best when a helpful side pocket such as safety or trust is filled at the same moment. A listener who feels threatened has the main pocket reshaped shut so the same words that would bind in a calm moment slide past. That gives a concrete reason why care and safety have to arrive before a difficult idea can take hold and why a person who has food and shelter and someone who listens is far more able to take in something new.
