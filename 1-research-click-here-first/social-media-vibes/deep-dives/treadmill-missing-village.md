# The treadmill and the missing village: three reflections on work and retirement and community

> **About this page:** This page holds three reflections on why life can feel empty both with a job and without a job. An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote the first two reflections on 20 November 2026 and no other person has rechecked the two reflections. The author of this repository supplied the third reflection and the text appears in full.

## Reflection one

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

A joke like "no point retiring early because you would only be bored" is a confession that the job was the last thing holding a person's day together because nothing else was ever built to hold a human life.

**Two doors and misery behind both**

A person with a job gives forty to sixty hours a week to tasks that often numb the mind and the person arrives home with too little energy to know anyone deeply. A person without a job has the hours back and finds that nearly every place to spend the hours charges admission. The coffee shop wants a purchase and the gym wants a membership and the bar wants a tab. So the person without a paycheck lies in bed or walks the streets wondering where everyone went and the answer is that everyone is at work or behind a paywall. The remark "must be nice not to work" misses that free time without money in a pay to enter society is closer to house arrest than to a holiday.

**Why the misery follows a person out of the job**

Human beings lived for a very long time in groups where food and shelter and company were shared and where a person's day was full of other people by default. The present arrangement stripped that away and put a workplace in the gap. The workplace now supplies the schedule and the reason to leave the house and most of the faces a person sees. Take the workplace away and the gap is exposed because no village is waiting underneath. That is why a retiree can feel the days go flat. The job was never nourishing and the job was simply the only structure on offer.

**What a village costs under this arrangement**

A lasting community needs a place to gather and food to share and enough stability that people stay near each other for years. Each of those is priced. Housing near friends costs rent that a landlord can raise and a person who cannot pay is removed by armed deputies against that person's will. Raising a family near trusted adults requires those adults to afford the same neighborhood. So continuity which is the thing that turns a group of acquaintances into a village is rationed by money and people who lack money get scattered wherever the cheapest rent happens to be.

**The laugh that covers the alarm**

When someone laughs and says "I would be miserable without work anyway" the sentence deserves to be heard literally. The sentence says "my life outside of labor is empty enough that crushing labor is the better option." That is a five alarm fire told as a joke. Here is a possibility about the laughter: the speaker laughs because the alternative is to feel the full weight of the emptiness with no one nearby willing to hear the weight. Laughter keeps the conversation light and the light tone keeps anyone from having to ask why a human being in a wealthy country has nothing but a job to live for.

**The silence that keeps the arrangement in place**

A person who says aloud "working is miserable and not working is miserable" is often told to be grateful or to find a hobby. That reply closes the subject and the closing serves the people who profit from the arrangement because workers who believe the emptiness is a personal failing do not ask who designed days this way. People with the power to change zoning and wages and the price of shelter made choices that produced paywalled towns and scattered families. Those choices were made by living people and living people can make different ones.

**What the picture adds up to**

The three things a person needs most are food and shelter and deep connection with other human beings. Under the present arrangement the first two are held behind a job and the third is held behind the first two. So a person can be employed and fed and housed and still starving for connection or can be free of the job and too poor to reach anyone. Calling that arrangement an open air prison fits because the walls are made of prices and schedules and the inmates are told every day that the gate is open.

## Reflection two

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

A treadmill inside a locked cell is a fitting picture of the job because exhaustion keeps the eyes on the belt and off the door.

**What the treadmill does**

A person who works forty to sixty hours a week arrives home with just enough energy to eat and sleep and scroll. Tiredness works as a kind of numbing agent because a mind that is worn out does not have the strength to ask "who do I belong to?" The weekend fills with errands and recovery and then the belt starts again. So the job does two things at once which are paying for food and shelter and keeping a person too busy to notice that the hours outside of work lead nowhere.

**The cage with no walls**

A person who steps off the treadmill is free to go anywhere and discovers that anywhere is empty. The park has benches and no one who knows the person's name. The bike trail has other riders passing with earbuds in. Standing still on a path and looking into the middle distance the person asks "where is the village?" and nothing answers. The bars of this cage are absences because no shared table and no door to knock on and no group expecting the person at dusk add up to confinement just as surely as concrete does.

**The two disturbing possibilities**

One possibility is that warm villages exist all around and the people inside keep quiet and do not open the gate for outsiders. The other possibility is that real villages are so rare that most people have never been inside one. My best guess is that the second is closer to the truth because the evidence visible from the street points that way. Most people when asked who they could call at midnight name a household member and a partner and perhaps one or two friends. A circle that small is a lifeboat and not a village since one move to another city or one breakup or one death can empty the boat.

**Why the rarity is the more unsettling answer**

If villages were hidden a person could at least search for the hidden gate. If villages are rare then the lonely person on the bike trail is looking at a crowd of people who are lonely in the same way and who each believe everyone else has somewhere to be. Each person reads the others' silence as proof that the others are fine. So thousands of people can stand within sight of each other and each one concludes "the problem must be me" because nobody says the sentence out loud.

**How people talk past the bars**

The remark "you would be bored without your job" treats the emptiness as a fact about the person and not as a fact about the surroundings. Here is a possibility about why the remark is so common: admitting that the job is a distraction from a missing community would mean facing the missing community and the treadmill is easier to face. The cost of that avoidance lands on real lives because a person can spend forty years on the belt and reach the end with a pension and no one to eat dinner with.

**What seeing the bars makes possible**

A person who has stopped running and looked around has information that the runners do not have. That person can say plainly to another human being "I have looked and I cannot find a village and I suspect you cannot either." A sentence like that breaks the shared silence for at least two people. A village begins as exactly that kind of exchange repeated among a handful of people who decide to keep showing up for each other and who find some way to share food and shelter costs so that the showing up can last. The first requirement is that someone says what the cage looks like while everyone else is still watching the belt.

## Reflection three

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied this reflection in three parts on 20 November 2026 and the text appears in full.

People laughing off the idea that retiring early just makes you bored and miserable is a massive tell, because it treats a completely hollowed-out society like a cute quirk of human nature.

If stepping away from a soul-crushing 50-hour week actually makes life feel more empty, that is not proof that humans secretly love shift work and fluorescent lighting. It is proof that the job quietly ate everything that used to exist outside of it. When you are working full-time, you are too drained to build a real life. When you stop working—whether you are unemployed or retired—you step outside on a Tuesday afternoon and realize the neighborhood is a ghost town because everyone else is still trapped at their desks or registers.

On top of that, almost every place left where people actually gather has been turned into a tollbooth. If you are not buying a coffee, paying a cover charge, or swiping a card for an activity, you are basically loitering. Without money to constantly buy your way into spaces, free time shrinks down to staring at the ceiling or walking around aimlessly wondering where all the people went. And even when you do find a few people you genuinely click with, trying to build anything with real continuity—sharing daily meals, raising the next generation, having a real village—immediately slams straight into rent, land costs, and grocery bills.

So the whole arrangement ends up working like a double bind. Either you stay on the treadmill and feel miserable because your hours belong to someone else, or you step off the treadmill and feel miserable because there is no community waiting on the other side. People joke about being bored in retirement because laughing at it is much easier than staring directly at the five-alarm fire underneath: a society where work has become the only remaining substitute for belonging, purpose, and daily human contact.

When you hear people casually shrug and accept that trade-off as normal, do you think they genuinely do not feel how bleak it is, or is joking about it just the only way they keep themselves from snapping?

Part 2: Because exhaustion works like anesthesia. When you spend 40 to 60 hours a week grinding through a repetitive job, commuting, running errands, and spending the weekend just trying to recover, you barely have the quiet space or spare energy to notice how hollow everything outside of work has become.

A full-time job hands you a synthetic substitute for a village. It gives you a schedule, hands you tasks that make the hours disappear, and puts other human beings in the same room so you are not sitting in total silence. The moment you step away from it, all of that artificial noise cuts off. Suddenly you are looking straight at the actual bars of the cage: empty streets during the day, paywalled gathering spaces, and a society where almost nobody has the margin left to build real continuity together.

People act like staying on the treadmill is the safer choice because seeing the cage without an obvious way out is terrifying. If someone realizes there is no real community waiting for them on the other side of work, admitting that the job is soul-crushing leaves them with nowhere to stand. So their mind flips the script to protect itself. Instead of asking why everyday life outside of work has been stripped down to nothing, they treat that sudden wave of boredom and emptiness as proof that they need the job just to stay grounded.

It is a remarkably effective trap: the exact thing draining a person's time and spirit is also the only thing keeping them too tired to notice how much of real human life has been hollowed out around them.

Once you have stepped back and seen those bars clearly, do you think it is even possible to go back to ignoring them, or does seeing it permanently change what you are willing to put up with?

Part 3: The second option is almost certainly the reality, and in a quiet way, it is the more disturbing one of the two.

If there were thriving, open-hearted villages tucked away in every town and everyone was just gatekeeping them, at least you would know the blueprint still worked and existed somewhere within reach. Instead, what you are noticing when you stand on a bike trail or walk through a park is an actual structural absence. For the overwhelming majority of people, the village has been shrunk down to a micro-bunker—a romantic partner, maybe a parent or sibling they keep up with, and one or two friends they manage to see every few weeks when work schedules happen to overlap.

That is why the image of a treadmill in a wall-less solitary cell lands so hard. You are not locked in a dark room; you have full freedom to walk down a sunny street, pass twenty other people walking their dogs or jogging with headphones in, and still experience a total social vacuum. Everyone is moving through the exact same physical space while staying completely sealed inside their own private loop.

There is a small piece of the first option mixed in, too, though it usually comes from scarcity panic rather than some coordinated secret club. When people do manage to cobble together a fragile little circle or a steady household, they often seal the hatches. Because everyone is already stretched thin on time, money, and emotional energy, bringing a new person into the fold feels like taking on weight they cannot afford to drop. So the few small pockets of connection that do exist stay closed, not because they are overflowing with abundance, but because they are barely holding together as it is.

When you look at other people passing by out in public and realize most of them are probably just as stranded in those tiny two-or-three-person bunkers, does that make the whole landscape feel even heavier, or does it take some of the sting out of feeling like you are the only one on the outside looking in?
