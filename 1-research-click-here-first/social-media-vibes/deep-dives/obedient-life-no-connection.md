# The obedient life: following every rule and ending up known by no one

> **About this page:** This page holds two reflections on a person who follows every unwritten rule of polite society and reaches the end of life without family or community. An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote the first reflection on 20 November 2026 and no other person has rechecked the reflection. The author of this repository supplied the second reflection.

## Reflection one

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

A person who obeys every unwritten rule of polite society can reach the end of a long life having bothered no one and having been known by no one because almost none of the rules point toward other human beings.

**The rules one at a time**

Each rule sounds reasonable alone. Say yes when the manager asks for overtime. Keep the home tidy. Pay the landlord on time. Do not burden others with personal troubles. Smile and nod. Do not knock on a neighbor's door uninvited. Give to charity. Never ask for help because asking is an imposition. A person can follow each rule and feel like a good citizen at every step.

**The rules added together**

Stack the rules and a full life appears. The mornings and afternoons go to an employer. The evenings go to laundry and dishes and repairs. The weekends go to errands and recovery. The remaining minutes go to a screen where advertisers and announcers suggest where to send money next. Nothing on the list says "sit with another person for three hours and learn what frightens that person and what that person hopes for." So the obedient life is a life of transactions with employers and stores and landlords and almost no hours of the one thing that makes a human being feel real to another human being.

**Who each rule serves**

- "Say yes to extra work" serves the people who profit from the extra hours.
- "Pay on time and never complain" serves the people who collect the rent.
- "Do not bother anyone" serves everyone who would prefer not to be asked for anything.
- "Give generously and ask for nothing" moves money in one direction and keeps need out of sight.

None of the rules serves the person following the rules in the three places that matter most which are secure food and shelter and deep connection. A rule set that benefits the people being obeyed and drains the person obeying is a rule set worth questioning out loud.

**What the body and mind pay**

A person who says yes to every request carries years of tension in the chest and shoulders and sleeps too little. A person who goes weeks speaking only to cashiers and supervisors can start to feel unreal or numb because a mind built for company is running without company. Here is a possibility about where that road leads for many people: illness that arrives early and a slow flattening where joy and anger both go quiet. The person did nothing wrong by the rulebook and the rulebook has no line for "this person is wasting away from being alone."

**The rule about never asking for help**

The cruelest rule is the one that forbids asking. A person may give for decades and be thanked each time and the same person learns that requesting anything in return is considered rude. So the giving builds no bond because a bond requires need flowing in both directions. The person ends up surrounded by people who received and by nobody who knows the person well enough to notice the person struggling. "Thank you for your generosity" followed by a closed door is a receipt and not a relationship.

**The ending the rulebook produces**

The obedient person dies somewhere between sixty and eighty with the bills paid and the lawn trimmed. The employer posts the job. The landlord lists the unit. The charity removes a name from the mailing list. Very few people can say what the person loved or feared because nobody was ever invited close enough to find out and the person was taught that inviting someone close was an imposition.

**What the picture reveals**

A set of norms that can be followed perfectly all the way to a lonely death was not designed around human flourishing. The norms were shaped by living people over time and many of the norms happen to keep workers working and tenants paying and needs unspoken. A person who breaks the quiet rules by saying "I am lonely and I want to know you" or "I need help this month" is not being rude. That person is doing the thing the rulebook left out which is treating another human being as someone to be known and not as a party to a transaction.

## Reflection two

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied this reflection on 20 November 2026.

That happens because modern societal norms are not actually a blueprint for human thriving—they are a blueprint for being a low-maintenance, frictionless component.

Look at the common thread running through every single rule you just listed. Say yes to every work request. Pay the landlord on time without a fuss. Keep up with endless chores and errands. Buy your food politely from corporate storefronts. Stay inside, stay quiet, never bother anybody, and definitely never ask anyone for help. Every single one of those expectations is engineered to maximize what flows out of you—labor, rent, money, compliance—while reducing to zero what anyone else has to invest back into you.

Real human connection is the exact opposite of low-maintenance. Genuine closeness requires interrupting each other, being inconvenient, needing favors, and spending hours that cannot be billed or optimized. Because polite society treats being a burden as one of the worst sins you can commit, following every norm to the letter forces you to sand off every rough edge that would normally lock you into a real community. You end up filling your waking hours with inanimate tasks, screens, and sterile transactions just to avoid stepping on anyone's toes.

Even the push to donate to charities fits that exact same mold. It takes your natural human desire to care for other people and funnels it into a neat, impersonal wire transfer. You hand over your spare money, get a cheerful automated receipt thanking you for being a good person, and stay safely isolated in your own apartment. And the moment you are the one who actually needs a hand, the polite warmth evaporates, because the entire script only works smoothly when the energy flows one way.

So when a diligent person follows every single instruction and winds up wrecked by stress, hollowed out, or quietly losing their mind at seventy with nobody around, that is not an accident or a personal failure. It is the exact finish line of a system that measures a good citizen by how reliably they work, pay, stay quiet, and eventually clear out without ever jamming the gears.

When you look back at how many of those rules get hammered into people from early in life, which one do you think does the most damage—the pressure to always say yes to work, or the unspoken rule that asking another human being for help is rude?
