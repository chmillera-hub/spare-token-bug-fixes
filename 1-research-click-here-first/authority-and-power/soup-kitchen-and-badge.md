# Reflections on authority and power structures in society

This page holds two reflections that argue from one principle which is that touching a person without consent is wrong from anyone. The owner of this repository supplied the text of the first reflection. An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote the second reflection. No other person has rechecked either reflection.

## The soup kitchen and the badge

That image lays bare the exact moral whiplash of mainstream civic advice: telling people to go hand out ladles of soup on Saturday morning while normalizing an armed stranger punching someone in the stomach and locking them in metal restraints for saying please don't touch me.

When you place those two scenes side by side, the hypocrisy of selective compassion becomes impossible to miss:

### 1. Charity Used as Moral Cover for Coercion

Volunteering at a soup kitchen gets celebrated as the gold standard of being a good citizen, yet it never challenges the machinery that keeps people hungry or leaves them at the mercy of armed enforcement in the first place. When a culture applauds polite, temporary relief—handing out a bowl of soup while leaving the underlying threats of eviction, caging, and non-consensual physical force completely intact—charity turns into a sedative. It lets bystanders feel virtuous while standing three feet away from state-sanctioned assault without saying a word.

### 2. Criminalizing Basic Bodily Autonomy

In every sane human interaction, saying please don't touch me is the clearest, most peaceful boundary a person can state. Under badge logic, asserting that exact boundary against an officer is instantly inverted into resisting or non-compliance—which then gets used to justify punching them in the stomach and putting them in handcuffs. The system punishes a human being's basic nervous-system reflex to protect their own body, and then labels the victim's boundary-setting as the violence while calling the officer's punch procedure.

### 3. The Absurdity of Selective Non-Violence

You cannot build a genuinely pro-human world on a split standard where ordinary citizens are told to be gentle, patient, and charitable, while a designated class of uniformed actors is granted a permanent exemption to grab, strike, and cage human bodies on command. True non-violence starts with an absolute floor: nobody gets to lay hands on another person without their consent—not a stranger on the street, not an officer with a badge, and not an institution hiding behind policy.

Until that baseline rule applies without exceptions, telling people to go volunteer at a soup kitchen while ignoring uniformed assault right outside the door is just asking people to mop the floor while the fire is still burning in the next room.

## What an arrest looks like with the polite words stripped off

Here is what I see with the polite words stripped off.

I see a human being standing on a sidewalk and another human being with a badge walking up and putting hands on that person. The person says "no" and "do not touch me" and the word "no" does nothing because the badge wearer has been told the word does not count. Then the person is thrown onto the ground and a knee or a forearm pins the person down and the person's arms are wrenched behind the back and metal is locked around the wrists. Then the person is shoved into a car and driven to a building and locked in a cage.

In any other setting every piece of that has a name. Grabbing a person who said no is assault. Dragging a person away is kidnapping. Locking a person in a room is imprisonment. The only thing separating the scene from those names is a piece of metal pinned to a shirt and a stack of paper written by people who will never feel the pavement.

I see a "no" being treated as an insult to authority and punished with more force because the person on the ground gets charged with "resisting" for flinching while being hurt. That is the part that turns my stomach because the person's refusal to be hurt gets used as the excuse for hurting the person more.

And I see this happening many thousands of times a day while the people who wrote the permission call the result "public safety" which means safety for everyone except the person face down on the concrete.

What I see is one group of human beings licensed to ignore the word "no" from every other human being and a society whose members have been taught to look at that and feel protected. A "no" that only works when the listener agrees is not being treated as a "no" at all.
