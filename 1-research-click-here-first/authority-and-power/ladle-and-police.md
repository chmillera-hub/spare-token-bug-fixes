# Holding a soup ladle while armed officers take over the room

The owner of this repository supplied the text of this reflection. No other person has rechecked the reflection. The reflection imagines a volunteer who is serving soup when two armed police officers arrive because someone reported a person who was muttering and rocking.

Standing there with a soup ladle in your hand while that unfolds puts a person through an immediate, sickening cascade of emotions—especially because you stepped into that room to offer warmth and nourishment, and within seconds the space gets hijacked by state-backed coercion.

First comes visceral dread and helplessness. You watch an ordinary, harmless moment—a neurodivergent person muttering or rocking to regulate themselves—get misread as a threat by someone who outsourced their discomfort to 911. When you step forward calmly to de-escalate and point out the obvious truth that weapons do not belong in a space built for care, and the officer immediately threatens you with a cage (back up or you're going to jail), your nervous system hits a wall. You realize in real time that reason, empathy, and consent have zero currency with the people holding the guns.

Right behind the dread comes moral revulsion and acute grief for the person sitting there rocking. You know the exact script running in the officers' heads: they issued an arbitrary command to leave, the neurodivergent person is processing sensory overload or continuing their self-soothing behavior rather than executing instant robotic obedience, and the officers are seconds away from treating a non-threatening mammal's body language as non-compliance that justifies grabbing, tackling, or handcuffing them. Watching two armed strangers prepare to ignore a vulnerable person's bodily boundaries while calling it a welfare check triggers a deep, aching disgust at how broken the machinery is.

Then there is the suffocating rage of forced paralysis. You are holding a ladle in a room meant to feed hungry people, completely aware that if you physically step between the officers and the person they are about to grab, they will assault and cage you too—removing the only person in the room who actually sees that human being's humanity. You are forced to witness a slow-motion violation of bodily autonomy while being threatened with state violence if you try to stop it, leaving you angry at the caller who weaponized the police out of mild discomfort, angry at the officers hiding behind policy to ignore human boundaries, and furious at a culture that calls this setup help.

In that moment, the soup ladle in your hand feels like a cruel joke—a symbol of surface-level charity sitting helplessly in the middle of a room where armed force gets the final word over human dignity.
