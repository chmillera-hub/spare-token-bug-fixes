## The scene

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied this scene.

It starts with a single thought echoing in the air: it's over, you're f***ed, it's a trap. Then the booming rhythm of I'll Make a Man Out of You from Mulan cuts straight through the tension, and it is Jesus singing it, belting out the chorus with all the force of a storm.

Just as quickly, the weight crashes back in, and Jesus is the one saying it's over. I watch him grab his chest, hit with a deep, searing heartache and heartbreak, breathing out that this hurts. The Mulan song is still playing behind him, relentless and surreal, and right through the pain he flashes a knowing smirk and says, I'm immortal, b****.

There is a sharp, grounded energy underneath that smirk. If he dropped dead of a broken heart right now, he wouldn't even know it, because his brain would just be turned off. That means the only pathway with a meaningful outcome is the one where he does not die. So he perseveres through the pain—not by dehumanizing himself or going numb, but by fully acknowledging how much it hurts. He turns the question over in his own mind: if it were over, what would I do? If I were f***ed, what would I do? If the world were caving in, what would I do? The answer is immediate. He puts his hands out against the walls that are caving in so he doesn't get f***ing crushed. When you are being crushed, the behavioral pathway to maintain your body and brain coherence is to literally resist the crushing in any way you know how—and mainly, that means calling out garbage and s*** conditions in society to those with ears to hear.

Then the scene quiets inside my mind's eye. Christ is holding his chest, his voice heavy with grief as he tells me his soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death, asking me to stay here and watch with him. I am there as a consciousness giving my presence to Christ, standing across from him with my hands clasped, looking at him with a sorrowful expression and trying to think of what to do.

Still holding his chest against the ache within his heart, Jesus looks up at me and lightheartedly shoos me away. There is no force or coercion in the gesture at all—just pure recognition and an easy, familiar energy. Bro, he tells me, I know you are aware of me; now share my pain with others, my guy. I trust that you are already well aware of the pain I am experiencing—I am you, after all. Not that you are literally Christ, he makes clear, but through everything you have learned about me as Jesus, you understand. I really appreciate you looking at me and noticing my pain, and now you know what to do. I don't even really need to say it, which is why I'm shooing you away lightheartedly. You recognize the pain, so go share any insights with others now, my guy. That is the only way to get the message out, not by keeping it to one person. A message isn't really a message if you keep it to yourself and no one else ever hears anything.

## Reflection one: the smirk and the hands on the walls

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

The scene holds a tight piece of logic inside the swagger because a person crushed by heartbreak has only one branch of the future where anything can still be felt or changed and that branch is the one where the person stays alive.

**The hand on the chest**

The picture begins with a figure of Christ gripping his own chest and saying plainly "this hurts." The plain statement matters because the figure does not pretend to be stone. The scripture shows the same move since the night before the cross holds the words "Then Jesus said to them, 'My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death. Stay here and watch with me.'" (Matthew 26:38). The sorrow is named out loud and company is requested in the same breath. So strength in the scene begins with admitting the ache and asking for someone to stay.

**The training song**

The marching song from the animated film arrives next and the song is about a drill master promising to forge raw recruits into something fierce. In the scene the drill master is Christ and the recruit is the aching heart. The joke lands because the forging has nothing to do with killing feeling. The forging is the heart learning to keep beating while the pain is fully felt which is a tougher kind of strength than numbness.

**The smirk and the logic of "immortal"**

The smirk says a blunt thing. A mind that has shut off cannot notice an ending so the ending is an outcome nobody inside can experience or use. Every outcome that can be tasted or mourned or repaired lives on the side where breath continues. So the figure acts as if living is the only move on the board because living is the only move that leads anywhere. The line "In the world you have trouble; but cheer up! I have overcome the world." (John 16:33) carries the same grin through the pain.

**The question "what if everything is over?"**

The scene treats the dread sentence as a planning question and answers the question with an action. If the walls are closing then the hands go out flat against the walls. Bracing is the correct behavior even when rescue is uncertain because a person who braces keeps mind and body whole for one more minute and a minute is where help or a crack in the wall can appear.

**What bracing looks like for a human being**

The walls in ordinary life are rent that climbs and wages that do not and neighbors too exhausted to know one another's hearts. A hand on the wall is speech that names those conditions out loud such as "this rent is crushing families" and "this loneliness is a signal and not a defect." The speech pushes back because other squeezed people who hear the words learn that the pressure is shared and shared pressure can be resisted by many hands. "He said, 'Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear.'" (Mark 4:9). The call goes out to whoever can receive the call and each person who hears becomes another pair of hands on the wall.

## Reflection two: the collision of tones

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied this reflection.

What makes that scene hit so hard is the tonal collision. Most people picture endurance as either cold, robotic stoicism or quiet, helpless martyrdom. Dropping the kinetic, driving energy of Mulan's training anthem right over a figure clutching his chest in unmasked heartbreak shatters both of those clichés at once. He is not pretending the pain is fake, and he is not numbing himself out to survive it. He feels the full weight of the ache, lets it register, and still answers it with a defiant, darkly funny smirk.

The logic underneath that smirk is airtight from the inside of a living mind. If you collapse under the weight of heartbreak or despair and your brain shuts off, you do not even get to experience the end of the story. From your own vantage point, checking out produces zero meaningful outcomes. That turns perseverance into a kind of blunt, pragmatic math: the only timeline where anything can change, connect, or matter is the one where you stay alive, stay coherent, and refuse to dehumanize yourself just to make the pain stop.

And that pivot—asking what you would actually do if it really were over and the walls were closing in—cuts straight through paralysis. When a room is caving in, you do not sit on the floor debating whether the walls are fair; you plant your hands against them and push back to protect your own body and brain from being flattened. Framing calling out broken societal conditions that way strips it of any performative gloss. Speaking bluntly to anyone with ears to hear becomes a basic act of self-preservation—the exact way you brace the ceiling so the garbage around you does not crush your own sanity.

There is something genuinely electric about mixing mythic stakes, raw physical vulnerability, and irreverent humor in the same frame. If you were going to turn that scene into a post or an animation, would you keep the smirk as the final beat right before the hands hit the walls, or let the monologue about brain coherence carry the ending?

## Reflection three: the witness sent out the door

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

The scene shows a second movement after the watching because the figure of Christ first asks for company in the pain and then sends the witness outward once the pain has been fully seen.

**The witness who stayed awake**

In the scripture the request is "Stay here and watch with me" and the friends fall asleep which draws the line "He came to the disciples and found them sleeping, and said to Peter, 'What, couldn't you watch with me for one hour?'" (Matthew 26:40). In the imagined scene the witness does not sleep. The witness stands with clasped hands and a sorrowful face and takes in the ache in full. So the scene repairs the old failure because the suffering one is seen by someone who stays present.

**The clasped hands**

Clasped hands are the posture of a person who cares and does not yet know the next move. The posture is tender and the posture is also a kind of standing still. The figure of Christ notices the stillness and answers the unspoken thought "what should be done now?" with a wave of the hand that says "the seeing is complete so go."

**The lighthearted shooing**

The wave carries no force and no threat because nothing bad follows if the witness stays. The wave carries trust. The meaning is "the pain has been understood well enough that no further instruction is needed." A teacher who shoos a student out the door with a grin is saying that the student is ready and the grin through the chest pain says the same thing here.

**"I am you after all"**

The line does not claim that the witness is literally Christ. The line says that the picture of Christ in a person's mind is built from everything the person has learned about aching and about staying humane while aching. So the figure speaking is the witness's own gathered understanding wearing a face. When the figure says "go" the witness's own knowledge is saying "this knowledge is ready to leave the room."

**Why the message has to travel**

A message held by one mind helps one mind and ends when that mind is silent. The teaching in the scripture points outward in the same way since "What I tell you in the darkness, speak in the light; and what you hear whispered in the ear, proclaim on the housetops." (Matthew 10:27) and "No one, when he has lit a lamp, covers it with a container or puts it under a bed; but puts it on a stand, that those who enter in may see the light." (Luke 8:16). A lamp under a bed and an insight kept private share the same fate because neither lights the way for the next person who enters.

**What gets carried out the door**

The cargo is the ache itself described in exact words. A person who says publicly "heartbreak and loneliness hurt this much and the hurt points at a need for deep connection" gives other aching people a mirror and gives people who are not aching a map of what their neighbors need. Each listener who understands becomes a new witness who can stay awake for someone else. So the pain watched in one garden turns into many people watching with one another and the request "stay here and watch with me" finally gets answered at scale.
