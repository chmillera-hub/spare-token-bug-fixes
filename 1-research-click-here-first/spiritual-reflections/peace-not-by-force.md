# Peace is not reached by force: four reflections on the arc from the Old Testament to the Gospels

## First reflection: how much of the Old Testament teaches that force fails to bring peace

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

A large share of the Old Testament carries the lesson that a person or a nation that uses violence to get a peaceful and flourishing world ends up without that world. A rough estimate is that well over half of the story material shows violence failing to deliver peace while a smaller and troubling share reports violence as commanded or celebrated.

### Where the lesson shows up in the stories

- **The first killing.** The first brother to kill a brother gains nothing he wanted because he ends as a wanderer cut off from the soil and from family.
- **The flood.** The reason given for the flood is that the earth was filled with violence so the text names violence as the thing that ruins a world.
- **The shepherd king.** The most celebrated king arranges the death of a loyal soldier to take the soldier's wife and afterward his own household tears apart through assault and rebellion and the deaths of sons. The same king is told he may not build the temple because he shed much blood which means the text treats bloodshed as disqualifying even in a hero.
- **The book of tribal chiefs.** Each rescue by force buys a short quiet followed by a worse collapse and the book ends in civil war and atrocity so the cycle demonstrates that the sword did not produce lasting safety.
- **The kingdoms.** The histories of the two kingdoms read as a chain of plots and assassinations in which those who seize a throne by killing are killed in turn and the chain ends with both kingdoms destroyed and the people marched away.

### Where the lesson is stated outright

Psalm 7:16

> The trouble he causes shall return to his own head. His violence shall come down on the crown of his own head.

Isaiah 59:8

> They don’t know the way of peace; and there is no justice in their ways. They have made crooked paths for themselves; whoever goes in them doesn’t know peace.

Isaiah 2:4

> He will judge between the nations, and will decide concerning many peoples. They shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks. Nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.

Psalm 37:11

> But the humble shall inherit the land, and shall delight themselves in the abundance of peace.

The first two verses describe the mechanism which is that violence circles back onto the one who used violence. The last two verses describe the goal which is a world where weapons become farm tools and people have enough food and quiet to flourish.

### The part that cuts the other way

The conquest accounts report whole towns killed under divine command and some psalms ask for enemies to be crushed so a reader cannot truthfully say the whole collection condemns violence. Readers have handled those passages in different ways: some take the commands as literal history and others read the commands as a people's own war stories placed in the mouth of God and still others note that the later books answer the earlier ones by showing where conquest led. The collection itself supports that last reading in one respect because the land taken by the sword is the same land lost to the sword by the end of the histories.

### The overall arc

Read from front to back the collection moves from a garden with enough food for everyone through centuries of killing to prophets who say the killing must stop so the long plot works like a demonstration by exhaustion. Every violent route to security gets tried such as strong kings and big armies and alliances with empires and every route ends in famine or exile. The lesson a reader can carry away is that a person or a nation that reaches for violence to obtain peace receives more violence and that the peace described by the prophets arrives through sharing food and land and through refusing to learn war.

## Second reflection: the Old Testament as a case study lasting many centuries

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied the text of this reflection and an AI chatbot (Claude) reworded the text at the request of the author.

A very large part of the Old Testament reads like a case study lasting many centuries because the main story line from Genesis through the Prophets keeps showing the same result. The text holds plenty of brutal war stories and the long-term outcome of using violence to secure peace is collapse in nearly every case.

- **The loop of escalation in Genesis.** Cain uses violence to answer jealousy at the very start and within a few generations his descendant Lamech boasts of killing a young man merely for bruising him. The story then explains the ruin of the world before the Flood as the earth being filled with violence and not as a shortage of rules.
- **The strongman trap in Judges.** The book of Judges is a bleak repeating cycle of tribal war and private payback and military strongmen. Each time someone uses brute force to fix a crisis the relief fades and the next round of violence is uglier until the last chapters describe a senseless civil war that nearly wipes out a whole tribe.
- **The cost of the sword under the kings.** When the people ask for a king to fight battles and make everyone feel safe the prophet Samuel warns plainly that a king will take sons and daughters for war and for service and will take grain. David is the most celebrated warrior king in the text and David is barred from building the temple because he has shed too much blood. Once David uses the power of the throne to have Uriah killed at the front violence breaks out inside the household of David and does not leave.
- **The prophets answering the empires.** Isaiah and Jeremiah and Micah and Amos spend page after page taking apart the belief that armies or economic pressure produce stability because those prophets keep saying that cities built on bloodshed and on squeezing workers rot from the inside. The picture of human flourishing in those books is the reverse of conquest: swords are beaten into plowshares so that metal goes toward growing food and each person sits in safety under a vine and a fig tree with nobody causing fear.

A fully stated account has to add that the Old Testament is not a tidy manifesto against all fighting because parts of Joshua and the other conquest accounts present tribal violence as triumphant or as backed by God. A reader who steps back and follows the whole story from start to finish still finds that the sword does not deliver the peaceful and thriving world that people keep hoping for since the sword buys a brief pause before the next round of revenge or corruption or exile.

One way to hold both parts together is to read the conquest passages as human beings placing their own drive for tribal survival in the mouth of God and to read the prophets as the voices that finally say the whole violent strategy is a dead end. Other readers take the conquest passages as commands that were given in fact and the text itself does not settle which reading is right.

## Third reflection: the Gospels as the proposal to try nonviolence

> **Who wrote this:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this reflection.

The Gospels present a teacher who names violence as the dead end and who is then killed by the people whose power rests on violence.

### The proposal

Matthew 5:44

> But I tell you, love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you,

Matthew 26:52

> Then Jesus said to him, “Put your sword back into its place, for all those who take the sword will die by the sword.

The second verse is spoken at the moment of arrest when a follower draws a blade against the armed party so the teaching is applied at the exact point where self-defense would seem most justified. The sentence also restates the lesson of the older histories in one line since those books showed throne after throne taken by the sword and lost by the sword.

### The response from people with power

The religious council and the governor's soldiers answer with arrest and flogging and public execution and the stated charge is a political one which is claiming a kingship that rivals the emperor. That charge shows what the authorities heard in the teaching: a way of living in which people share bread and forgive debts and refuse to kill leaves rulers with nothing to threaten because the tools of rule are the soldier and the cage and the cross.

### Why the execution supports the point of the teaching

The story has the most peaceful figure in the narrative killed by the most respected institutions of the day so the execution exposes the violence sitting at the top of the social order under words such as law and order. A reader watching a healer nailed up by officials can no longer assume that official violence lands only on dangerous people. The teacher's reply from the cross is forgiveness and not a curse which means the cycle of injury and payback is refused even at the cost of life.

### Two refinements to the picture

- **The prophets had already said it.** The call to beat swords into plowshares is in the older books and the teacher quotes those books constantly so the Gospel is better described as the proposal of the prophets finally acted out in one life than as a new idea replacing a failed old one.
- **The New Testament is not of one voice either.** One letter tells readers "Don’t be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good." and a chapter later describes the ruler as bearing the sword and many readers have used that later passage to defend police and armies. The last book also pictures a final battle in fierce imagery. The claim that the teaching forbids violence by governments and soldiers is therefore a well-supported reading of the teacher's own words and actions and other readers have long disagreed.

### What the work of nonviolence looks like

The earliest communities described in the text answered with practice and not with a revolt: members pooled food and property so that nobody went without and members refused to return blows. That practice addresses the reason people accept rulers with swords in the first place which is fear of going without food or shelter because people who share food and housing with each other have less need of an armed protector. The method is slow since the method spreads one table at a time and the method costs something since the people who tried the method were often jailed or killed by officials.

### The line running through both collections

The older books run the experiment of seeking peace through force and record the wreckage and the Gospels run the experiment of refusing force and record the price. Put together the two collections hand a reader a choice with the evidence attached: violence promises safety and delivers more violence while shared food and shelter and deep knowledge of one another deliver peace and require courage to begin.

## Fourth reflection: why officials felt they had to execute a nonviolent teacher

> **Who wrote this:** The author of this repository supplied the text of this reflection and an AI chatbot (Claude) reworded the text at the request of the author.

The two collections form one arc in two parts. The Old Testament runs the trial of violent kingdoms and strongmen for centuries and shows the trial ending in blood and collapse. The New Testament then brings in a completely different way of operating which is to refuse the sword and give people food freely and take apart the fear that keeps everyone obedient and the people at the top of the pyramid react with alarm at once.

The execution of Jesus is revealing because of the reason Roman officials and local leaders felt they had to kill him. The rulers of an empire are well prepared for a violent rebel because rulers know the answer to a person who picks up a sword against the state: the rulers send a bigger army and crush the rebel and use the spectacle to argue that brute force is the one language that works.

A radically nonviolent central figure breaks that script. When a person refuses to fight back and refuses to bow to contests of status and heals the ear of the armed guard who arrived to make the arrest the officials can no longer shelter behind the claim of public safety. By taking the full weight of an execution ordered by the state without striking back Jesus made the people at the highest levels of government and policing and military power show their hand in daylight. The execution exposed that the soldiers and courts were serving the control held by rulers and were not serving the safety of ordinary people.

Jesus was not naive about how much effort breaking the cycle would take because he understood that violence was locked in at the very top of society. He therefore did not offer nonviolence as an easy or instant fix and offered nonviolence as the one exit that does not simply replace one violent empire with a slightly different violent empire under new management.

One of the sharpest ironies in human history may be how quickly later governments and armies took the name of a man executed by the state for nonviolence and painted that name onto shields and flags. The irony matters to a reader today because a name on a flag proves nothing and the test of any use of the name is whether the people using the name give food and shelter and care or bring soldiers and cages.
