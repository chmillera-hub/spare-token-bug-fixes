# Spin rates: how fast a ribbon loop would need to circulate for each mass

November 15 2026

## The question

The main document in this folder says that mass could have two sources which are the squeeze of the inward-flowing Higgs field and the spinning of the Mobius strips inside a particle or a knot. This page puts rough numbers on the spinning source. The page asks how many laps each second a ribbon would need to make around a loop for the energy of the motion to equal the measured mass of a particle. The page then asks whether a longer strip that holds more energy lets atoms of different sizes share one spin rate.

Every number on this page is a sample calculation and is not a test of the ribbon picture because each calculation starts from assumptions that nobody has checked against an experiment.

## How laps each second relate to the speed of light

Every calculation on this page assumes that the ribbon travels around the loop at the speed of light which is about 300 million meters each second. The distance around the loop times the laps each second therefore always equals the speed of light. The lap counts look enormous because the loops are tiny and not because any part of a ribbon moves faster than light.

Light circles the whole Earth about 7.5 times each second because the Earth is about 40 million meters around. A track the size of the core of a hydrogen atom is about 4 followed by 22 zeros times shorter than the Earth is around so the same light-speed traveler finishes about 4 followed by 22 zeros times more laps in the same second.

A ribbon moving slower than light could not do the job on a small loop because a solid hoop as small as the largest size that collision experiments allow for an electron would need an edge speed about 190000 times the speed of light to carry the measured spin of an electron.

## First calculation: all of the mass from spinning

The first calculation gives the ribbon no energy of its own which means all of the mass has to be the energy of the circulating motion. The loop is round and the ribbon moves at the speed of light and the loop carries the amount of spin that experiments measure for these particles which physicists call one half unit of spin. Those assumptions fix the size of the loop and the lap rate with no free choices left.

The rule that falls out is that a heavier particle needs a smaller loop and a faster lap rate because the lap rate grows in direct proportion to the mass.

- Neutrino with an assumed mass of 0.05 electron volts: radius 2.0 x 10^-6 meters and 2.4 x 10^13 laps each second.
- Electron: radius 1.9 x 10^-13 meters and 2.5 x 10^20 laps each second.
- Muon which is a heavier cousin of the electron: radius 9.3 x 10^-16 meters and 5.1 x 10^22 laps each second.
- Proton treated as one loop: radius 1.1 x 10^-16 meters and 4.5 x 10^23 laps each second.
- Tau which is the heaviest cousin of the electron: radius 5.6 x 10^-17 meters and 8.6 x 10^23 laps each second.
- Top quark which is the heaviest known basic particle: radius 5.7 x 10^-19 meters and 8.3 x 10^25 laps each second.

The widest strip that each loop could hold is the distance around the loop divided by 1.732 because the page named smallest-size-finding.md in this folder reports that a paper Mobius strip has to be more than 1.732 times as long as wide. The electron loop could therefore hold a strip up to about 7 x 10^-13 meters wide.

## Where the first calculation meets measured physics

- The two-lap rule lands on a known number. A Mobius strip needs two laps to return right side up so the full-return rate is half the lap rate. The full-return rate for the electron is 1.24 x 10^20 each second which equals the internal frequency that standard quantum physics assigns to an electron (the mass energy of the electron divided by the quantum constant). The match holds for every mass because the two-lap rule cancels a factor of two in the formula.
- The proton does not fit inside one loop. The measured radius of a proton is about 8.4 x 10^-16 meters which is 8 times the radius of the single loop. The gap could fit the picture of a proton as a knot of several strips because a bundle of strips takes more room than one loop.
- The electron loop is far too wide for collision experiments. Collision experiments find no structure inside the electron down to about 10^-18 meters and the electron loop is about 200000 times wider than 10^-18 meters. The ribbon picture would need a reason why collisions see a point when the loop is so wide.

## Second calculation: a longer strip that holds more energy

The second calculation gives every stretch of strip a fixed amount of energy which means a longer strip is a heavier strip. The amount used is a measured number from the strong force which is the force that holds the pieces inside a proton together. The stretched strong-force field between the pieces inside a proton stores about 0.91 billion electron volts for every femtometer of length. A femtometer is a millionth of a billionth of a meter. The mass energy of one proton is about 0.94 billion electron volts so the measured number is roughly one proton of mass for every femtometer of strip.

Strip length needed for the core of each atom:

- Hydrogen: about 1 femtometer.
- Helium: about 4 femtometers.
- Carbon: about 12 femtometers.
- Iron: about 57 femtometers.
- Uranium: about 244 femtometers.

## Three shapes compared

- Shrinking loop with no energy in the strip which is the first calculation: hydrogen needs 4.5 x 10^23 laps each second and uranium needs 1.1 x 10^26 laps each second. Uranium must lap 236 times more often than hydrogen.
- One big open loop of long strip: hydrogen makes 2.9 x 10^23 laps each second and uranium makes 1.2 x 10^21 laps each second. Uranium laps 236 times less often than hydrogen because the speed is capped at the speed of light and the track is longer.
- Long strip coiled around a loop the size of the hydrogen loop like rope wound into a coil: every atom from hydrogen to uranium makes 2.9 x 10^23 laps each second because every turn of the coil is the same size. Uranium is then about 234 turns of the same loop.

The coiled shape is the shape that lets atoms of different sizes share one spin rate because the mass rises with the number of turns while the lap rate stays the same.

## Where the second calculation meets measured physics

- The mass of the core of an atom grows in step with the count of protons and neutrons in the core which matches mass growing in step with strip length.
- The core of every atom has about the same density which fits a fixed amount of strip for each unit of room.
- A real family of heavy short-lived particles shows spin rising with the square of the mass which is the pattern that one spinning stretched strip produces.
- Physicists find that the Higgs field supplies only a small part of the mass of a proton while most of the mass of a proton is the energy of the motion and binding of the pieces inside the proton. That measurement fits a picture in which most of the mass of an atom sits in strip energy and motion.

## Where the second calculation strains

- A coil with every turn circulating the same way would give uranium about 234 times the spin of hydrogen. The measured spin of the common forms of carbon and iron and uranium is zero. The coil would therefore need neighboring turns that circulate in opposite directions so the spins cancel in pairs. Measured protons and neutrons do pair up with opposite spins inside the core of an atom.
- One big open loop fails more strongly. The spin of one big loop grows with the square of the mass and the uranium loop would be about 39 femtometers in radius while the measured core of uranium is about 7.4 femtometers in radius.
- The electron and the muon and the tau carry the same spin at very different masses. A longer strip alone cannot explain those three particles without the same kind of canceling.

## What stays unproven

- Nobody knows whether a ribbon of space carries energy for each unit of length. The second calculation borrowed the measured number from the strong force and a ribbon of space could have a different number.
- The calculations did not include the squeeze of the inward-flowing Higgs field which is the other source of mass in the main document. A share of mass from the squeeze makes the loop wider and the lap rate lower by the same share. A proton with half of the mass from spinning needs a loop twice as wide that laps half as often.
- The way a coil could make neighboring turns circulate in opposite directions is still to be determined.
- The measured values on this page such as the radius of a proton and the size limit for the electron are standard published values that the chatbot recalled and did not look up for this page.
- No other person has rerun the programs which means every number from the programs is unconfirmed.
