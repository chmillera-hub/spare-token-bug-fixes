# Master list of 293 equations ranked by prime percentage

> **About this page:** One AI model (Claude) ran the computations and wrote this page in a single chat on 21 November 2026. No other person has rerun the work yet which means the results are unconfirmed by a human reviewer. Each percentage comes from a computer count and not from a written proof.

## What the list shows

The list ranks 293 equations by how many primes the outputs contain compared with plain counting numbers of the same size. The top of the list holds the equation with the most primes and the bottom of the list holds the equations at −100% which means the equations that produced no primes.

+859% means the equation produced 859% more primes than the number line because the computer counted the primes among the outputs and divided by the count that chance predicts for ordinary numbers of the same sizes.

The page [grouped-list.md](grouped-list.md) holds the same equations sorted into groups by type of equation.

## The rules behind every line

An output with decimal places has the decimal places dropped with no rounding (1.99 becomes 1) unless the line names a different rounding rule.

"Bump the last digit" means the last digit of each output moves up to the nearest of 1 or 3 or 7 or 9 so 0 becomes 1 and 2 becomes 3 and 4 or 5 or 6 becomes 7 and 8 becomes 9.

A prime that an equation outputs more than once counts once so an equation that keeps repeating the same prime gets a low percentage.

Each equation was tested on 300000 inputs with outputs under 1 trillion unless the line says otherwise. A line says "rough" when chance predicts fewer than 50 primes because luck can then move the percentage by a large amount.

## What stands out

The highest percentage under 1 trillion belongs to n² + n + 26853927251 at +859% because no prime below 97 can divide any output and 39% of the 300000 outputs tested were prime. A computer search covered every equation of the form n² + n + A with A below 900 billion.

No highest possible percentage exists because multiplying n by the product of every prime up to some prime p and adding 1 blocks every prime up to p from dividing an output. The percentage keeps climbing as p grows (+457% for p = 17 and +726% for p = 97 and +1030% for p = 541 and +1108% for p = 1009) although the climb slows down and the outputs grow to hundreds of digits.

Trig functions and square roots and π and e and logarithms land near 0% because the dropped decimals scramble the outputs so each small prime divides the outputs at the ordinary rate.

A rounding rule can change a structured equation a lot. For example n² ÷ 10 sits at −44% with decimals dropped and at +73% when any decimals round up. Dropping the decimals subtracts the remainder and the remainder of a square is often a square such as 1 or 4 or 9 and a square minus a square splits into two factors. Rounding up adds a number and a square plus a number does not split that way.
