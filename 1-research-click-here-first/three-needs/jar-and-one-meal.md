# What a jar of marbles says about one meal

> **About this page:** An AI chatbot (Claude) wrote this page on 22 November 2026. No other person has rechecked the page. The author of this repository asked how a long chat about logarithms and prime numbers reflects on society and this page is the answer. The math behind each paragraph sits in the question pages of the folder [prime-richness](../prime-richness). Each comparison between numbers and people is a picture offered for reflection and not a proof.

The math keeps landing on one idea that also describes how people treat one another: the same single thing matters enormously to a person who has almost nothing and barely at all to a person who already has plenty.

**One more marble.** Plain counting adds one marble to a jar each time. Measured against what the jar already holds the second marble is a gain of 100% and the thousandth marble is a gain of a tenth of a percent. One meal works the same way because a meal handed to a person with empty cupboards is the whole day of staying alive while the same meal handed to a person with a full pantry changes nothing. People who set wages and rents and food prices usually count in raw totals. A stomach counts in percentages because hunger measures a meal against what is already inside.

**Rates add and hours do not.** Two people painting one fence finish sooner than the faster painter could alone because during every hour both brushes are moving. Cooking and cleaning and keeping a home running follow the same arithmetic. When people who live close together do that work during the same hours the load on each person drops below what the strongest person could carry alone. A person living alone carries every hour of every task with nobody else's rate added in.

**The smooth curve cannot see one number.** The rule for how often primes appear is an average drawn through a jagged record of yes and no. The rule gives the same answer for a prime and for the non-prime number next door. A figure such as average income is the same kind of smooth curve. A person removed from an apartment by armed deputies and sleeping outside in the cold is one jagged point that the curve does not show. People who make decisions from the curve alone are deciding without seeing that person.

**One large piece and a few crumbs.** A typical number is built from one huge prime that holds about five eighths of the number plus a few tiny primes. The prime 2 touches half of all numbers and still holds almost none of any number's length. That reads as a picture of human contact. Brief contact is everywhere and holds little. A bond where two people know each other's heart and mind is rare and holds most of what a life is made of.

**Early help grows and late help does not.** Growth delivered in one lump at the end falls short of the same growth spread across the whole stretch because growth that arrives early starts growing too. Food and shelter that reach a person early give that person something to build on. The same food and shelter after months outdoors reach a mind and body already worn down by cold and hunger.

**The luck test.** Every count on the prime pages got checked against one question: could plain luck explain the result? Few people ask that question before deciding that a person without food or housing earned the outcome.

**Shared knowledge multiplies.** About four hundred years ago one person spent twenty years computing a table of logarithms by hand and printed the table. Sailors and astronomers who had never met that person were spared years of labor. Knowledge written plainly and given away keeps multiplying because each reader can pass the knowledge to the next.
