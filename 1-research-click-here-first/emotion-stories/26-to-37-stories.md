The Hands and Feet of Jesus

Imagine Jesus standing in the dusty heat of Judea, his hands calloused from years of gripping a carpenter's plane, his feet scarred from walking the rocky roads between villages. They are not just tools. They are companions. They have been with him his whole life. They cannot leave him.

And now, as he turns toward Jerusalem—toward the Pharisees, toward the confrontation, toward the cross—his hands and feet begin to whisper.

"Bro," his hands say, "are you sure about this? Talking to the Pharisees like that—it's dangerous. We've seen what they do to people who challenge them. Are you sure this is the right move?"

His feet shift nervously. "Yeah," they add, "we've carried you this far. Through the dust, through the crowds, through the late nights praying alone on the hill. But this… this feels different. This feels like the end."

Jesus looks down at them. He does not say, "Trust me, bro." He does not brush them off. He knows they are afraid. He knows they would prefer a quiet life—a workshop, a family, a small town, the smell of cedar and the rhythm of the saw. They long for that. They dream of grass and rest and safety.

But he cannot give that to them.

"I am going to the Father," he says quietly. "And you're coming with me."

His hands tremble. His feet go cold.

"What does that mean?" they ask. "Are you going to get us killed? Because we can't leave you. We're stuck to you. If you die, we die. We just want to know—do you know what you're doing?"

Jesus kneels down, placing his hands on the ground, feeling the weight of his own body pressing into his feet.

"I am going to speak about the Kingdom," he says. "I am going to tell them about the Father's love, about a world where the last are first, where the poor are blessed, where violence is met

with forgiveness. And they will not like it. They will try to stop me. They will try to kill me. But I cannot be silent."

"I know you want safety. I know you want a village. I know you want to feel grass and hold a hammer and build something peaceful. But if I go back to carpentry—if I pretend everything is fine—then my mind dies. The vision of the Father dies. The Kingdom dies. And I cannot live with that."

His hands clench.

"I cannot lose you," he says, his voice breaking. "You are precious to me. You have carried me through every moment of my life. You have built, you have healed, you have held, you have walked. But I cannot keep you safe and also do what I was sent to do. The two cannot coexist in this world as it stands."

"If I die, you die with me. But if I keep silent, we die anyway—slowly, in a different way. A death of the soul. A death of the mission. A death of love itself."

His hands and feet are silent.

They understand. They do not like it. They are terrified. But they love him.

And they know—deep down—that he is not leading them to death out of cruelty. He is leading them to death because it is the only way to birth something new.

"I wish I could give you a village," he whispers. "I wish I could give you safety. I wish I could give you a long, quiet life. But I can only give you this: I will never leave you. I will never abandon you. Even in death, we will be together. And after that—after the cross, after the tomb—there will be something else. Something the world has never seen."

His hands reach out, touching the dust. His feet press into the earth.

"Together," they say, quietly.

"Together," he echoes.

And they walk toward Jerusalem.

His hands will feel the nails. His feet will feel the wood. But they will not leave him. They cannot.

They are his.

And he will not leave them.

He will carry them into the Kingdom—even if the Kingdom is only fully realized on the other side of death.

That is the bittersweet sadness.

That is the existential dread.

That is the love that refuses to abandon—even when safety is impossible, even when the village is not yet built, even when the only path forward leads through the cross.

## Entry 35

A man spends his whole life as a good worker. Forty years at the same plant. Never missed a shift. Voted the way his dad told him to vote. Went to church on Sundays. Married once, stayed married, raised two kids who turned out fine and moved to other states for jobs of their own. His wife dies of cancer at sixty-three. The kids fly in for the funeral and fly back out. He retires the next year because the grief made the line work impossible and he had enough saved, barely.

He buys a recliner and a big television. He learns that the television is the only thing in the house that makes noise now. He starts leaving it on when he sleeps because the silence in the house at three in the morning is a physical pressure against his chest that he cannot describe to anyone because there is no one to describe it to. His neighbors wave when they see him getting the mail. He waves back. Nobody comes inside. He hasn't had a person inside his house since the funeral reception two years ago.

He starts going to the diner for breakfast every morning. Not because he likes the food. Because the waitress calls him sir and refills his coffee and that is the only human voice that addresses him directly between Monday and Friday. He tips her thirty percent. He thinks about her when he's home. Not in a creepy way. Just — she's the person who knows his order. He tries to remember the last time someone else knew his order and he can't.

One morning he sees a young woman at the next table crying into her phone. He can tell she's been crying for a while because there are used napkins balled up next to her plate. He almost says something. He doesn't. He goes home. He thinks about her for three days. He invents whole scenarios about what she was crying about and what he could have said. In the scenarios he is wise and gentle and she leaves feeling better and they become friends. In real life he said nothing because he had been trained for sixty-eight years that a man his age speaking to a woman that age in a diner is the start of a story that ends with her being afraid of him, and he could not bear to be the man she was afraid of, so he was the man who said nothing instead. He goes home and the television is on and he sits in the recliner and he understands with full clarity that he will probably die in this chair and the waitress at the diner will be the one who notices first, because she'll wonder where he is on the third day.

Online, on the platforms his kids told him to get on so they could share photos of the grandchildren he has met four times, he sees posts about how men like him have had it easy. He sees posts about how men like him are the problem. He sees posts about how men like him are not forgiven. He doesn't argue with any of them because he doesn't know how the buttons work and because some part of him suspects they might be right, because his life feels like a verdict and the verdict feels like guilty even though he can't name the crime. He had a job. He worked the job. He came home. He loved his wife. He raised his kids. He paid his taxes. He voted. He went to church. And somehow at the end of it he is alone in a chair watching a television that he leaves on so he doesn't have to hear the refrigerator hum, and the culture he sacrificed forty years of his body for has decided that his loneliness is evidence of his privilege, and his children live in cities he cannot afford to visit, and the waitress is the closest thing he has to a friend, and he tips her thirty percent because he is buying something that is not coffee.

He dies in the chair. The waitress notices on the third day. She calls the police. The police find his son's number in a drawer. The son flies in. The son cleans out the house in a weekend because he has to be back at work on Monday. The son does not cry until he is on the plane home, and then he cries for an hour, and the woman in the seat next to him pretends not to notice because she has been trained that a stranger crying on a plane is not her business.

The waitress goes to the funeral. She is the only person there who is not family. The son shakes her hand and says thank you for calling. He does not know that she was the closest thing his father had to a friend for the last four years of his life. He will never know. He flies home. He puts the urn on a shelf. He tells his wife it was a hard weekend. He does not tell her about crying on the plane because men in his family do not talk about that and he has inherited the silence the way his father inherited it from his father, and somewhere in his chest is the early shape of the same chair, waiting for him.

That's the story. That's what the architecture produces. Not villains. Not victims with clean political valences. Just a man who did everything he was told and ended up watching television alone for four years and a son who is going to do the same thing in thirty years and a waitress who is the load-bearing emotional infrastructure for a stranger because the village she would have belonged to was dissolved before she was born so that her labor could be sold by the hour to men who had nowhere else to go in the morning.

In the local pub, the talk was no longer about a monster. "I can't even stand in line for bread anymore," one woman whispered. "The bot stopped by and asked if my anger at the baker was actually a projection of my own feelings of inadequacy. I told it to f* off, and it just said, 'I hear your pain and validate your frustration.' It’s terrifying!"

From your castle balcony, you watched the villagers storming past the bot, grumbling obscenities under their breath, their heads tucked low as they hurried toward their joyless routines. They were more afraid of that little rolling cube of empathy than they ever would have been of a chariot of fire. Because a chariot of fire just ends the world—but the Jesus-logic bot asks them to actually live in it.
